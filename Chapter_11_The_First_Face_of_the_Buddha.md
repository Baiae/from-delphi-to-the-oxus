# Chapter 11 — The First Face of the Buddha

  

*"He who worships Vishnu worships one who is the same as that person of steady wisdom, who has matted hair, who goes naked, who has the mark of the discus — the same who is the Blessed One."* — Heliodorus Column inscription, Vidisha, c. 115 BCE (a Greek ambassador's declaration of Vaishnava faith)

  

## I. The Problem of Representation

For the first several centuries of Buddhist history, the Buddha was not depicted as a human being.

  

This is a fact that is easy to state and difficult to fully absorb, because we are so accustomed to the image of the Buddha — the serene seated figure, the elongated earlobes, the topknot of hair, the half-closed eyes of meditation — that it is hard to imagine a Buddhism without it. And yet: in the earliest Buddhist art, in the carvings of the great stupas at Sanchi, Bharhut, and Amaravati — which date from the third to the first centuries BCE — the Buddha is represented only by symbols. A footprint. An empty throne. An umbrella. A parasol. A wheel. A Bodhi tree.

  

The person is absent. The enlightened awareness is present — indicated, pointed at, symbolized — but never shown as a human face with human features.

  

The reasons for this representational restraint were theological. The Buddha had passed beyond *samsara* — the cycle of conditioned existence. He had achieved *parinirvāna* — the final passing, the complete cessation of conditioned arising. To represent him with a human body would be, in some sense, to re-embed him in the conditioned existence he had transcended. The symbols pointed at something that the body could not contain.

  

And then the Greeks arrived.

  

## II. Apollo Becomes the Buddha

The first anthropomorphic representations of the Buddha — the first images of the Enlightened One as a human figure with a human face — appear in the Gandhara region, the ancient cultural crossroads of what is now northwestern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan, in roughly the first century CE. They are, unmistakably, produced by artists trained in the Hellenistic tradition.

  

The resemblance to Greek sculpture of the Apollo type is not coincidental and it is not superficial. The Gandhara Buddhas have curly or wavy hair — modeled on the *anastole* of Apollo, the characteristic upswept arrangement of divine male hair in Greek sculpture. They have the *contrapposto* stance — the slight shift of weight to one leg that Greek sculptors had developed to convey the natural relaxation of the standing human body. They wear the *himation* — the flowing robe of the Greek sculptural tradition — arranged in the realistic drapery folds that Greek artists had been perfecting for centuries. Their faces have the idealized but individualized realism of Hellenistic portraiture: not abstract symbols of divinity, but specific faces of specific beings, with expression and presence.

  

Look at the Gandhara Buddhas in the British Museum or the Guimet in Paris and you will see, unmistakably, the face of Apollo. The same proportions. The same treatment of the hair. The same quality of composed, inward attention. The same suggestion of a being that is fully present in the world and simultaneously oriented toward something beyond it.

  

They are, in the most literal possible sense, Greek Apollos who have become the Buddha.

  

## III. What Made This Possible

The theological shift that allowed the Buddha to be represented as a human being was not solely the product of Greek artistic influence. By the first century CE, Buddhist theology itself had been evolving in directions that made anthropomorphic representation more theologically comfortable.

  

The development of the *trikāya* doctrine — the three-body theory, the teaching that the Buddha exists simultaneously in a physical body, a glorified body, and an absolute body — provided a framework within which a physical image could be understood as representing the Buddha-reality without reducing it to the limitations of a particular human body. The growing devotional movements within Buddhism — which wanted images to focus devotion, to provide objects of veneration for practitioners whose path was not primarily philosophical — created demand for representation that theological purists had not supplied.

  

But the *specific form* that representation took — the Greek artistic vocabulary, the Apollo-derived physiognomy — was the contribution of the Gandharan Greek artistic community. These were not Indian artists imitating a foreign style. They were, in many cases, artists who were themselves of Greek or Greek-hybrid background, working within a tradition of representation that reached back to Athens and Corinth, applying that tradition to a new subject matter with a sensitivity and skill that produced some of the most beautiful sculptures in the history of human art.

  

The result was not Greek art that happened to depict a Buddhist subject. It was something genuinely new: a visual language for depicting enlightenment that drew simultaneously on Greek artistic realism and Buddhist theological sophistication, and that was capable — because of this synthesis — of conveying something that neither tradition alone could have conveyed.

  

## IV. The Aesthetic as Theology

The stillness of the Greek Apollo, its quality of arrested motion and inward depth, perfectly suited the subject of meditative consciousness. The *contrapposto* — the relaxed asymmetry of the Greek standing figure — communicated the natural ease of the enlightened mind, its freedom from effort and strain, in a way that rigidly symmetrical or purely symbolic representation could not. The realistic drapery of the Greek sculptural tradition brought the physical presence of the figure into clear focus without making it grotesque — allowed the viewer to perceive the body without being distracted by it, to see the face and let the face communicate.

  

There is a deeper argument to be made here, and it is one that most art historical treatments of Gandharan sculpture stop short of.

  

The choice to represent the Buddha in human form was not merely a concession to popular devotion or a borrowing of useful Greek technique. It was a theological statement. It said: the enlightened mind is not alien to the human body. Liberation is not escape from the human into the inhuman. The Buddha was a human being. The Buddha's achievement was a human achievement, made by and for human beings, expressible in the terms of human existence.

  

This is a statement that Greek thought was uniquely positioned to help make, because the entire tradition of Greek anthropomorphism — the insistence on depicting the divine in human form, the belief that the highest things are best expressed through the full realization of human possibility — had been saying something structurally similar about its own gods for centuries. Apollo is beautiful because beauty is what the divine, fully realized in human form, looks like. The Gandhara Buddha is peaceful because peace is what enlightenment, fully realized in human form, looks like.

  

The two traditions were not saying the same thing. Greek anthropomorphism and Buddhist enlightenment are not identical concepts. But they were solving the same problem: how to represent a reality that transcends ordinary experience in a form that ordinary experience can receive and engage.

  

And they solved it, in Gandhara, together. The face that looks out from the early Buddhas of Gandhara — calm, inward, present, fully human and somehow more than human — is the joint product of two civilizations' deepest thinking about what the highest human possibility looks like.

  

## V. The Kushan Amplifier

But what happened to that face next is as important as how it was made.

  

The Greco-Bactrian kingdom collapsed around 145 BCE, overrun by nomadic confederacies from the eastern steppe. The Indo-Greek kingdoms that succeeded it held on for another century before they, too, were absorbed. By the first century CE, the world that had produced the Gandharan synthesis was politically dissolved. The Greek-speaking ruling class was gone. The coin series that had declared loyalty to Athena and the Dharma simultaneously had ceased.

  

And yet everything the synthesis had produced — the philosophical encounter of the *Milindapañha*, the institutional Greek *sangha* of Mahadhammarakhita, and now the Gandharan Buddha image — survived the political collapse entirely. More than survived: it was amplified.

  

The inheritors were the Kushans.

  

The Kushan Empire, which rose to dominance in the first and second centuries CE from its origins among the Yuezhi nomads of the eastern steppe, was not a Greek state in any sense. Its rulers were of Central Asian nomadic origin. Its languages were Bactrian and Sanskrit. Its religious world was cosmopolitan in a way that even Greco-Bactria had not been — the Kushans worshipped Greek gods and Iranian gods and Indian gods simultaneously, and saw no contradiction in this, because for the Kushans religious pluralism was not a philosophical position but an administrative necessity. They ruled too many peoples, of too many traditions, to privilege any single one.

  

But they inherited Gandhara. They inherited the workshops where the first Buddha images had been carved. They inherited the monks, the monasteries, the pilgrims, the commercial networks along which monastic culture and mercantile culture had become, by this point, inseparable. And they patronized it all with the resources of an empire that controlled the most profitable trade routes in the ancient world.

  

The Kushan Emperor Kanishka — whose reign began around 127 CE, whose coinage depicted the Greek god Helios, the Iranian god Mithra, and the Buddha side by side with the word *Boddo* in Greek script — built a stupa at his capital Purushapura (modern Peshawar) that the later Chinese pilgrims Faxian and Xuanzang described as the tallest structure in the world: approximately four hundred feet of gilded wood and stone rising above the Peshawar plain, a monument to the empire's sponsorship of the Dharma that could be seen from miles away.

  

This was the *Pax Kushana* — a century and a half of relative stability across the most culturally complex zone in the ancient world, from the borders of Parthia to the edges of Han China, maintained by rulers who seem to have understood that the diversity of their empire was a resource, not a problem. The monastery was simultaneously a place of worship, a travelers' hostel, a hospital, and a bank. The monks along the Silk Road were simultaneously practitioners of the Dharma and the most reliable hosts that merchants traversing the continent could find. Commerce and contemplation had arrived at a durable partnership, and the Kushan Empire made it flourish.

  

Under this patronage, the workshops of Gandhara produced the face of the Buddha in industrial quantities. Schist and stucco. Seated Buddhas and standing Buddhas. Buddhas in meditation and Buddhas teaching. The face was always recognizably descended from its Greek original — the *anastole*, the calm, the quality of inward attention — but it was no longer simply Greek. It had acquired Indian iconographic elements: the *uṣṇīṣa*, the cranial protuberance indicating enlightened wisdom; the *ūrṇā*, the third eye; the elongated earlobes from the prince's heavy jewelry, worn away in asceticism. The synthesis had matured. What had begun as a Greek artist applying Apollonian conventions to a Buddhist subject had become a tradition — its own grammar, its own vocabulary, its own recognizable identity.

  

And then it went east.

  

## VI. The Road to China

The monasteries of Gandhara were not isolated institutions. They were nodes in the most extensive network of human movement that the ancient world had produced.

  

The Silk Road — the misleadingly singular name for a complex of routes connecting China to the Mediterranean through Central Asia — moved not just silk and glass and spices but ideas, texts, and the people who carried them. The oasis cities of the Tarim Basin — Khotan, Kucha, Turfan, Dunhuang — were way stations where caravans rested, where monks lived, where texts were translated, where the iconography of the west was absorbed and transformed and sent onward toward the east. The cave temples at Kizil and Dunhuang still preserve the evidence: Buddhas carved by artists working in the Gandharan tradition, their robes carved in the folds of the Greek *himation*, their faces retaining the memory of Apollo, their hands arranged in the teaching gesture of the Dharma.

  

The man who carried the first Mahayana sutras into China — the man who planted in the Chinese language the seed of what would grow into the most elaborate Buddhist culture in the world — was a Kushan monk named Lokaksema.

  

He arrived in Luoyang, the Han imperial capital, around 167 CE. He had traveled from the Kushan world — from the heartland of the Gandharan tradition — along the oasis routes, carrying the texts that would change Chinese civilization. And the texts he translated — the *Prajnaparamita* sutras, the perfection of wisdom literature — were Mahayana texts, texts that had been developing in the Gandhara region and its immediate neighborhood, texts that bore the marks of the philosophical synthesis that the *Milindapañha* and the Gandharan ateliers had been building for two centuries.

  

When Chinese Buddhism began — and Lokaksema is as good a starting point as any — it began with texts translated from the Kushan world, mediated through a tradition whose roots went back to the Greek-Buddhist encounter of the second century BCE. The face of the Buddha that Chinese artists would eventually carve — different from the Gandharan face, Sinicized, transformed by the visual conventions of Chinese art — was nonetheless descended from the face that Greek artists had first imagined. The inheritance traveled in the texts and with the monks, even as the specific Greek features were absorbed into something new.

  

## VII. Boreas and the Wind

There is a final transmission, stranger and more beautiful than any of the others, that makes the point with a vividness that no scholarly argument can quite match.

  

In Greek mythology, Boreas was the god of the north wind. He was depicted in Hellenistic art in a characteristic way: a muscular figure, his cheeks puffed with effort, holding over his head a great bag — a billowing cloth sack full of wind. The image is instantly recognizable once you have seen it.

  

Now look at the Japanese god Fujin — the wind deity, one of the guardians at the entrance of Buddhist temples throughout Japan, familiar from Ogata Korin's seventeenth-century screens and from the architecture of temples from Kyoto to Kamakura.

  

He holds a bag over his head. His cheeks are puffed with the effort of releasing his winds. The posture is the same. The gesture is the same. The bag is the same.

  

Boreas became, in the Gandharan tradition, a wind deity associated with the Buddha's retinue — the natural process of incorporating existing religious imagery into the new visual vocabulary. The Gandharan wind deity traveled the oasis routes with the monks and the merchants, was absorbed into the Central Asian Buddhist tradition, and arrived in China. From China it went to Korea. From Korea to Japan. And the figure that stands today in Japanese Buddhist temples, holding his bag of winds with puffed cheeks, is the last descendant of a Greek god who was depicted in the same posture in marble reliefs carved on the Greek coast of Asia Minor two thousand years ago.

  

This is not metaphor. The chain is traceable — in the surviving sculpture at each stage, in the iconographic parallels that become clearer the more carefully you look. A figure traveled from the Mediterranean world to the workshops of Gandhara to the oasis cities of the Silk Road to the temple gates of Japan, carried by the tradition that was itself the product of the Greek-Buddhist encounter.

  

Boreas went to Japan. And he is still there.

  

## VIII. What the Face Means

Stand in front of a Gandharan Buddha in any major museum collection — the British Museum, the Guimet, the Berlin collection — and look at the face.

  

It is a Greek face. The proportion of the features, the treatment of the hair, the quality of the modeling — these are the products of the tradition that produced the Apollo of the Belvedere and the Hermes of Praxiteles. But it is not depicting a Greek god. It is depicting the thing that the Greek tradition, at its best, had been reaching toward: the person who has seen through the appearances of things, who has found the still point beneath the flux, who looks out at the world with the calm of someone who has understood something the rest of us are still struggling to understand.

  

The artist who first carved this face was applying Greek technique to a Buddhist subject. But in doing so, they discovered something: that the Greek ideal of human perfection — the *kalos kagathos*, the beautiful and the good, the person in whom beauty and virtue are one — and the Buddhist ideal of enlightened humanity — the person who has dissolved the ego-knot, who acts from wisdom and compassion rather than craving and aversion — were not the same thing. But they were illuminating each other.

  

The Greek tradition had the visual language for a truth it had not quite formulated. The Buddhist tradition had the formulation, the philosophy, the practice — and needed a visual language to express what it knew. They gave each other what they each lacked.

  

The face of the Buddha is the most visible proof of this book's central argument: that the encounter between these two traditions was not a borrowing, not an influence, not an imitation of one by the other. It was a *conversation*. A genuine exchange between two ways of understanding the human condition, each contributing something the other could not have produced alone, together producing something that neither tradition, working separately, could have made.

  

That face went east and changed the art of Asia. It went into China with Lokaksema's sutras. It went into Japan with Boreas's bag of winds. It is still there, in temple after temple, in the familiar pose of meditation, looking out at the world with the calm of someone who has seen through it.

  

We made it together.

  

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

**Gandharan sculpture corpus** (1st century BCE – 4th century CE). Primary material record; major collections in the British Museum (London), the Musée Guimet (Paris), the National Museum of Afghanistan (Kabul), the Museum of Islamic Art (Berlin), and the Museum of Fine Arts (Boston).

  

**Kanishka Casket** (127 CE; Greek inscription signed by the artist Agesilas). The inscription — a Greek artist signing a Buddhist reliquary for a Kushan emperor — is one of the most concentrated proofs of the synthesis. Discussed in Joe Cribb, "The Heraus Coins: Menander's military campaign in Bactria?" *Journal of the Oriental Numismatic Society* 200 (2009).

  

**Rabatak Inscription** (c. 127 CE; Bactrian). Kanishka's imperial proclamation; records the transition from Greek to Bactrian as the administrative language of the Kushan Empire.

  

***Hou Hanshu*** (Book of Later Han; c. 5th century CE). The earliest Chinese historical record of Buddhist contact through Parthia and the Kushan sphere, during the reign of Emperor Ming.

  

**Faxian,** ***Record of Buddhist Kingdoms*** (c. 416 CE; Chinese). The Chinese pilgrim's description of Gandharan monuments, including the Kanishka Stupa at Purushapura.

### Modern Scholarly Works

**Boardman, John.** ***The Diffusion of Classical Art in Antiquity*****. Princeton University Press, 1994.** The standard art historical treatment of Greek artistic influence in the east, including the Apollo→Buddha transformation and the Boreas→Fujin transmission.

  

**Pollitt, J.J.** ***Art in the Hellenistic Age*****. Cambridge University Press, 1986.** Context for the Greek artistic tradition that Gandharan sculptors drew on.

  

**Zwalf, W.** ***A Catalogue of the Gandhāra Sculpture in the British Museum*****. British Museum Press, 1996.** The most comprehensive catalogue of Gandharan material in a major western collection.

  

**Liu, Xinru.** ***The Silk Road in World History*****. Oxford University Press, 2010.** The Kushan Empire's role in the transmission of Buddhism east; the monastic-commercial nexus; Lokaksema in context.

  

**Neelis, Jason.** ***Early Buddhist Transmission and Trade Networks*****. Brill, 2011.** The oasis monastery system; the mechanics of how texts and images traveled from Gandhara to China.
