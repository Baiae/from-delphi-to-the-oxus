# Chapter 8 — Translating the Dharma into Greek

  

*"In the ninth year of his reign, King Piyadassi, Beloved of the Gods, conquered the Kalingas... Thereupon, he became devoted to the teaching of Dharma, the love of Dharma, and the progress of Dharma."* — Ashoka, Rock Edict XIII, c. 257 BCE

  

## I. Tableau: Kandahar, c. 258 BCE

The building was nothing extraordinary from the outside — mud-brick, flat-roofed, designed for the Afghan sun, indistinguishable in its basic material from every other administrative building in a city full of administrative buildings. Inside, in the relative cool of the interior courtyard, two men were at work at adjacent tables. Both were literate. One wrote in Greek, in the careful block letters of official Hellenistic administration. The other wrote in Aramaic — the Persian imperial language that had survived the dissolution of the Achaemenid Empire and continued to function as the commercial and administrative *lingua franca* of the Iranian plateau, adopted by practically everyone who needed to communicate across the former Persian world without committing fully to Greek.

  

They were in Kandahar — ancient Arachosia, the city renamed after the conqueror whose campaign had passed through it almost seventy years earlier and whose influence had never quite left. Alexander had founded or refounded the city in 329 BCE during his eastern campaign; it had been Seleucid and then, after the treaty between Seleucus and Chandragupta, Mauryan. Now it was a provincial capital of Ashoka's empire — prosperous, multilingual, sitting at the crossroads of the Indian world to the east and the Hellenistic world to the west — and the two scribes in the administrative building had been given an instruction that had no good precedent in the history of human writing.

  

They were to take the emperor's edicts and render them into their respective languages for public inscription. The emperor's edicts concerned the moral life: how to treat parents, servants, animals, strangers. How to practice generosity. How to cultivate the inner life. Edicts that Ashoka had already had inscribed across the Indian heartland in Brahmi script, in Prakrit, for subjects who could read those languages. Now they needed Kandahar's population — Greek-educated bureaucrats and administrators on one side, Aramaic-speaking merchants and artisans on the other — to be able to read them.

  

They could translate almost everything. Instructions about respecting parents were universal. Generosity was a concept both languages held without difficulty. The condemnation of violence and the mandate to protect living creatures could be rendered in both scripts with reasonable fidelity.

  

And then they reached the central term.

  

*Dharma.*

  

The two scribes looked at each other across their tables — or perhaps they didn't; perhaps they simply sat in the absorbed silence of a professional problem too large for conversation — and the weight of the word settled over the room like the Afghan heat.

  

## II. The Word That Could Not Be Translated

In Sanskrit and Prakrit, *dharma* is one of the most semantically dense words in any language. A word that carries, simultaneously, multiple interlocking meanings that no single term in any other tongue fully captures.

  

*Dharma* is the cosmic order underlying the universe — the principle by which things are as they are, the deep structure of reality itself. It is the moral law — the right conduct appropriate to one's station, one's nature, one's particular web of relationships and obligations. It is the teaching — specifically, in Buddhist usage, the teaching of the Buddha, the Dharma as the second of the Three Jewels to which every Buddhist takes refuge. It is duty: what one owes to the world, to one's family, to one's community. It is righteousness, virtue, piety, right relationship. It is the way things ought to be.

  

All of this at once. All of this bound together, so that cosmic order and individual ethics and religious teaching and social obligation are not separate ideas that happen to share a word, but aspects of a single reality as understood by the tradition that developed this concept over three thousand years of serious thought.

  

The Greek philosophical vocabulary had many fine words. *Dikaiosunē* — justice. *Aretē* — virtue, excellence. *Nomos* — law, custom, convention. *Logos* — reason, word, principle. *Sophia* — wisdom. *Phronēsis* — practical wisdom, the knowledge of how to act rightly in particular circumstances. These were words of extraordinary precision and richness, the product of four centuries of Greek philosophical labour. None of them captured *dharma*. Not one, and not several in combination, because the problem was not one of vocabulary but of framework.

  

What made the translation problem so deep was not merely the linguistic gap. It was the gap between two different *diagnoses* of the human condition.

  

Greek philosophy, by Ashoka's time, had been largely preoccupied with a set of questions that we can group under the heading of knowledge: What is real? How can we know it? What is the relationship between appearance and reality, between what we perceive and what is actually there? This is not a trivial question. It is, in many ways, the foundational question of Western intellectual culture, and the Greek tradition pursued it with a rigour and a productivity that shaped every subsequent tradition that engaged with it.

  

The Indian tradition had been preoccupied with something more immediately urgent. Not "What is real?" but "Why do human beings suffer — and can it be stopped?"

  

The Buddha's answer to that second question was more specific, more clinical, and more practically actionable than anything the West had yet formulated for the same problem. Suffering — *dukkha*, the Pali word for which no adequate English translation exists — is not simply pain. It is not the ordinary suffering of physical injury or bereavement, though it encompasses those. The image embedded in the word is of a wheel with its axle off-centre: something that tears itself apart as it turns. What the Buddha had diagnosed was a fundamental structural mismatch in the human psyche.

  

We are creatures capable of genuine growth, love, and transformation — of becoming something more than we currently are, of entering into relationships of increasing depth and understanding, of developing wisdom that changes not merely what we know but who we are. And we persistently try to satisfy this capacity through acquisition. We try to *have* what can only be *become*. We try to possess love rather than embody it. We try to buy security rather than cultivate equanimity. We try to accumulate the outward signs of a wisdom we have not undergone. The wheel spins. It tears itself apart. And the tearing — the gap between what we need and how we try to get it — is *dukkha*.

  

*Dharma* was the teaching about the axle. The path by which the wheel is realigned. The cosmic and personal and ethical and soteriological principle that, when properly understood and practiced, does the work of ending the tearing.

  

The scribes in Kandahar needed to say this in Greek.

  

## III. Eusebeia

They chose *eusebeia*.

  

The word comes from two Greek roots: *eu* (well, rightly, properly) and *sebas* (reverence, awe, the feeling appropriate to the sacred). In its Greek context, *eusebeia* meant something like right piety — the proper disposition toward the divine, the correct observance of religious and moral obligations, the inward orientation that produced right outward behaviour. It was what you had when your relationship to what was ultimately real was correctly calibrated. It was not quite virtue in the abstract — *aretē* was the word for that. And it was not merely religious observance — *thrēskeia* was closer to that. *Eusebeia* was the alignment of the whole person — inward life and outward action — with what was finally and truly important.

  

It was not a perfect translation. No perfect translation was possible. *Eusebeia* lacked the cosmic-structural dimension of *dharma* — the sense of a universal ordering principle of reality itself. It lacked the soteriological precision of the Buddhist usage — the specific diagnosis of suffering and its remedy. It carried a more overtly religious connotation than *dharma* needed, and it lacked *dharma*'s resonance with social duty and role-obligation in the Hindu sense.

  

But it was an intelligent and honest choice. It captured the moral-religious quality of *dharma* — the sense of a right orientation toward something larger than the individual self, combining inward disposition with outward practice. And it pointed in the same direction: toward a way of being in the world that is simultaneously personal and cosmic, inner and outer, felt and enacted.

  

The scribes at Kandahar wrote *eusebeia* where Ashoka's Indian inscriptions wrote *dhamma*. And in doing so, they made a philosophical claim that went significantly beyond their translation mandate. They asserted that what the Buddhist emperor of the Mauryan Empire was prescribing for his subjects was recognizably commensurable with what the Greek philosophical tradition called right piety. That *dharma* and *eusebeia* pointed at the same territory. That a Greek reader who understood the full weight of *eusebeia* — who took seriously the Platonic and Stoic and popular-religious content of the word — was reading something that an Indian practitioner of *dharma* would recognize.

  

This was not a claim that the two traditions were identical. It was something more nuanced, more historically consequential: the claim that the traditions were *mutually legible* — that the deep pattern they were both tracing was visible from both sides, even if the paths to it were very different.

  

## IV. What the Edicts Actually Say

The Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription — excavated at modern Kandahar, now held in the Kabul Museum — is substantially legible in both its Greek and Aramaic columns, though damaged. What the Greek column contains is not a word-for-word translation of Ashoka's original Prakrit edicts but something closer to a philosophical restatement: the core moral teaching rendered in vocabulary and cadences that a Greek-educated reader would find natural.

  

The content concerns practical morality. Ashoka instructs his subjects in the renunciation of killing — animals as well as people. In tenderness toward living creatures. In respect for parents and elders. In generosity to servants, to the poor, to friends. In restraint from unnecessary violence and from the pleasures that cause harm to others. These are not specifically Buddhist doctrines in the technical sense — they are the broad *dharmic* ethic that Ashoka, in all his inscriptions, presents as the non-sectarian minimum: the behaviour that any reasonable human being, of any religious tradition, ought to be able to recognize as right.

  

But something extraordinary happens in the Greek version. The moral content, while universally recognizable, is framed in a way that would have been genuinely surprising to a Greek political imagination. A *king* is speaking directly to his subjects not about laws and punishments and civic duties but about the *inner life* — about the disposition of the soul, about what we would now call character formation, about the quality of attention and care that one brings to relationships. Greek political philosophy had its own account of the relationship between just laws and just citizens — Plato's *Republic*, Aristotle's *Politics*. But these were theoretical conversations among philosophers. A reigning monarch speaking directly to his people about how to cultivate their inner lives — not in the context of religious ceremony, not through the mediating authority of a priestly class, but as a direct act of royal governance — had no precedent in the Greek tradition.

  

What Ashoka had produced, inscribed in the Greek language on a rock face in what is now Afghanistan, was an argument that the inner moral life is the proper concern of governance. That the state exists not merely to manage competing interests and deter crime, but to *educate* its subjects in virtue — to actively participate in the formation of the human character of the people it governs. This was, as noted above, Plato's argument. The philosopher-king of the *Republic* governs not by force alone but by wisdom, and the purpose of his wisdom is to produce just citizens, not merely obedient ones. Ashoka had arrived at this conclusion through an entirely different route — not through Greek political philosophy but through Buddhist *dharma*, through the conversion experience of Kalinga, through the tradition of the Mauryan empire and its administrative sophistication — and he was now enacting it, in Greek, on stone, for the world to read.

  

The two philosophical traditions — Greek and Indian — had been converging on the same political insight. And here, on a rock in Arachosia, the convergence was being made public.

  

## V. The Five Greek Kings

There is another document from the Ashokan corpus that deserves more attention than it normally receives: Rock Edict XIII, the longest and most personal of Ashoka's surviving inscriptions.

  

The edict was composed around 257 BCE, in the aftermath of the Kalinga war — the campaign of conquest whose extraordinary violence had, by Ashoka's own account, triggered his conversion to Buddhism and his commitment to governing through *dharma* rather than military coercion. The edict describes the suffering the Kalinga war had caused, expresses Ashoka's genuine remorse, and announces his new political philosophy: conquest by *dharma*, not by force.

  

And then it names five men.

  

Antiochus II of Syria. Ptolemy II of Egypt. Antigonus Gonatas of Macedon. Magas of Cyrene. Alexander II of Epirus.

  

These are the five Hellenistic kings to whom Ashoka says he has sent his dharmic missions — ambassadors commissioned not to negotiate trade agreements or military alliances or territorial boundaries, but to spread the teaching of *dharma* in the Greek-speaking world. To go to the courts of the successors of Alexander and speak about right conduct, compassion for living creatures, respect for all religious traditions, and the inner life of the sovereign.

  

Consider what this means. Around 257 BCE, the emperor of the Mauryan Empire — the largest state in the world at that moment, governing a territory stretching from the Bay of Bengal to the borders of the Seleucid remnant states — sent diplomatic missions to five Greek courts with an explicitly philosophical mandate. Not armies. Not tribute demands. Not trade negotiations. *Philosophers of a kind*, bearing a moral teaching, addressing themselves to the intellectual and ethical life of the Mediterranean world.

  

We do not know, from the Greek side, how these missions were received. No Greek source records a Mauryan ambassador arriving at Alexandria or Pella with a message about *dharma*. The Hellenistic courts were busy with their own wars and successions and cultural projects; a foreign emperor's moral teaching may not have risen to the level of an event worth recording. But the missions were sent. The mandate was real. Ashoka spent the political and diplomatic capital required to reach across the known world with a philosophical message.

  

The Hidden Network, which had been carrying goods and ideas between these worlds for centuries, was carrying something new: a deliberate act of philosophical outreach from one great tradition to another, dispatched by a sovereign who believed that the wisdom of *dharma* was not merely a provincial Indian possession but a truth sufficiently universal to be offered to the Greek kings of the Mediterranean world.

  

Whether or not any Greek philosopher in Alexandria or Athens received and engaged with these missions, Ashoka believed they were worth sending. That belief tells us something essential about how he understood *dharma* — not as the ethnic or cultural property of the Mauryan world, but as something closer to what the Stoics were calling the *logos*: the rational principle underlying all things, accessible in principle to any mind capable of sufficient attention.

  

## VI. The Theory of the Reverse: Did Delphi Inspire Ashoka?

The scholar Valeri Yailenk has proposed something that cuts in the opposite direction from the story this chapter has been telling — and it is worth pausing to take it seriously, because if it is right (or even partially right), it deepens the picture considerably.

  

We have already encountered the Klearchos inscription at Ai-Khanoum (Chapter 5): the Delphic maxims, copied from the stone at Delphi and re-inscribed in the founder's sanctuary of a Greek city beside the Oxus, for the Greek and mixed-ancestry population of Bactria to read. Publicly inscribed. Addressed to everyone. On permanent stone surfaces in prominent locations. Concerning practical moral behaviour and the stages of the good life.

  

Yailenk's proposal: Ashoka may have seen something like this — or received reports of it — before he began his own project of public moral inscription. The Delphic maxim tradition, visible in the Bactrian Greek world throughout the third century BCE, may have been one of the inspirations for the formal structure of the Ashokan edicts: the idea of a king inscribing moral wisdom for public reading, on permanent stone, addressed to the ordinary person rather than to a priestly or scholarly elite.

  

The evidence is circumstantial. There is no document that says "Ashoka saw the Delphic maxims and decided to inscribe his own." But the formal parallels are precise enough to be uncomfortable with the word coincidence. The Ashokan edicts begin appearing in the 260s BCE — during or shortly after the period when the Delphic maxims were prominent in the Bactrian cities. Ashoka's diplomatic engagement with the Hellenistic world was active and continuous. He knew, in general terms, what the Greeks were doing.

  

What Yailenk's argument suggests is not that Ashoka copied the Greeks, but that the traffic was genuinely bidirectional. That Greek ideas about public moral instruction were crossing into the Mauryan political imagination at precisely the same moment that Indian ideas about *dharma* were being inscribed in Greek in frontier cities. That the cultural laboratory working at both ends of the corridor simultaneously — each tradition absorbing technique from the other while the deep content remained its own.

  

The Greek *form* of public moral inscription may have traveled east. The Indian *content* of *dharma* certainly traveled west. What emerged at Kandahar was neither Greek nor Indian but something the corridor was producing — translation as synthesis, the meaning-making that happens at the boundary between worlds.

  

## VII. The Semantic Bridge and Its Consequences

The choice of *eusebeia* had consequences that Ashoka's scribes could not have anticipated.

  

Because *eusebeia* was the word that the translators of the Hebrew scriptures into Greek — the project that produced the Septuagint, the Greek Bible that would become the theological foundation of early Christianity — chose for several of the Hebrew Bible's deepest moral concepts. Including *chesed*.

  

*Chesed* is to Hebrew moral theology what *dharma* is to Sanskrit: a word of extraordinary semantic density, untranslatable by any single English equivalent, carrying simultaneously the meanings of lovingkindness, covenant faithfulness, steadfast mercy, loyal love. It is God's disposition toward humanity — and, reciprocally, what God requires of human beings toward each other. It is not merely kindness in the ordinary sense. It is the quality of faithfulness that persists through difficulty and disappointment, the love that does not depend on the beloved's merit, the mercy that overflows the strict accountancy of desert.

  

When the Septuagint translators needed to carry *chesed* into Greek, they often reached for *eusebeia* and related terms — words in the *sebas* family, words of right orientation toward the sacred, words of proper relationship to what is ultimately real.

  

*Dharma. Eusebeia. Chesed.*

  

Three words. Three traditions. Three diagnoses of the deepest problem facing human beings — the problem of right relationship to reality, to each other, to whatever it is that grounds the moral order of the universe. Three traditions that had been developing their answers in isolation from each other for centuries. And all three of them, when they needed to speak across the boundary of their own language, reached for the same Greek word.

  

This is not coincidence in any meaningful sense. It is not that Greek just happened to have a versatile word that translators found convenient. It is that the problem all three traditions were solving — the problem of naming right relationship to what ultimately matters — constrained the solution in ways that made the convergence almost inevitable. The conceptual territory being mapped was the same territory. The maps used different symbols, had been drawn by different cartographers, organized the terrain according to different principles of importance. But they were all mapping the same mountains.

  

What Greek did, in becoming the language of *eusebeia*, was become the universal solvent — the medium in which three of the world's great wisdom traditions could, for a brief and extraordinary historical moment, be placed in the same sentence. Where an Indian Buddhist emperor's moral teaching became legible to a Greek philosopher's vocabulary. Where a Hebrew prophet's account of divine faithfulness dissolved into the same word that had carried the Buddha's teaching across the border.

  

The Kandahar scribes gave this convergence its first address. They inscribed it on stone, in two languages, on a rock face in what is now Afghanistan — where it waited two thousand years to be excavated, cleaned, and read again by a world that had largely forgotten it had ever existed.

  

But it had existed. It had been there, in the dust and the heat, since 258 BCE.

  

*Dharma. Eusebeia. Three traditions. One word. The same shore.*
