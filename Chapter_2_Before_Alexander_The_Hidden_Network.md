# Chapter 2 — Before Alexander: The Hidden Network

  

*"Then, there was neither existence nor non-existence. There was no air, no sky, no realm beyond... Who truly knows? From where did it come?"* — *Nasadiya Sukta*, Rigveda 10.129 (c. 1200–900 BCE)

  

## I. The Tablets from Persepolis

In 1933, a team of archaeologists from the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago was excavating the great terrace of Persepolis — the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, burned by Alexander the Great in a single spectacular act of revenge in 330 BCE — when their pickaxes broke through the mud-brick floor of a fortification wall and into a sealed chamber. Inside were clay tablets: tens of thousands of them, stacked in the darkness where the Achaemenid bureaucracy had placed them and left them when the palace fell. They had been baking slowly in the Persian sun for over two thousand years. They were, when retrieved, almost perfectly preserved.

  

It took decades to fully analyze the archive. The tablets were administrative records — the paperwork of an empire. Ration disbursements. Labor registers. Grain allocations. The prosaic machinery of the largest government the world had yet produced. A scholar could be forgiven, surrounded by this ocean of bureaucratic clay, for losing sight of the extraordinary picture it assembled.

  

But among the tens of thousands of tablets in what scholars now call the Persepolis Fortification Archive — the administrative records of the empire's eastern heartland dating from around 509 to 493 BCE — there was a detail of enormous significance. In the same columns, receiving rations from the same imperial granaries, laboring on the same construction projects at the same palace complexes, two categories of workers appeared with remarkable regularity: *Yauna* — Greeks — and *Hindush* — Indians. Side by side. In the same ledger. Administered by the same Persian bureaucracy, paid in the same standardized allocations of grain and wine and occasionally precious metal.

  

This is not metaphor. This is accounting.

  

The tablets record names and places. Greek workers from Ionian cities on the Aegean coast. Indian workers from the satrapy of *Hindush* in the Indus valley. Both groups serving, simultaneously, within the administrative structure of the same empire — an empire whose royal roads connected their distant homelands through a continuous infrastructure of way-stations, administrative posts, and Persian governors. Both paid in the same currency, governed by the same laws, required to communicate through the same bureaucratic language of Aramaic.

  

Before Alexander's armies crossed the Hellespont. Before the gymnosophists of the Indus encountered Greek philosophers for the first recorded time. Before any of the famous meetings of minds this book will document — Greeks and Indians were already sharing a workplace. They were already colleagues. They were already living within the same imperial system, breathing the same dust, eating from the same imperial granaries, working within earshot of each other on the palaces of Darius and Xerxes.

  

The wall between East and West, it turns out, was not merely a philosophical fiction. It was also, quite literally, a bureaucratic impossibility.

  

## II. The World That Made the Revolution

The sixth century BCE is one of the strangest periods in recorded history. Within the span of a single human lifetime, across civilizations with no demonstrable diplomatic contact, a cluster of ideas emerged that would define the intellectual trajectory of the human species for the next two and a half thousand years.

  

The Buddha attained enlightenment under a Bodhi tree in what is now Nepal or Bihar, and began teaching that suffering has a cause, that the self is not what it appears to be, and that liberation is possible. The Upanishadic sages — who had been developing their tradition for centuries — were in this same period formalizing the ancient Vedic intuition into a systematic metaphysics: that behind the multiplicity of appearances lies a single, ultimate reality — *Brahman* — identical with the ground of individual consciousness — *Atman*. In Ionia, on the Aegean coast of what is now western Turkey, Thales of Miletus proposed that water was the fundamental substance from which all things were made; Anaximander replaced water with the *apeiron*, the boundless and indefinite; Anaximenes proposed air; and Heraclitus of Ephesus — the most cryptic and brilliant of them all — said the answer was fire, and that behind the fire was something he called the *Logos*: a universal rational principle governing the ceaseless transformation of all things.

  

In Miletus and in Vaishali, in Athens and in Rajagriha, in Ephesus and in the forest hermitages of the Gangetic plain, human beings were simultaneously asking the same question: *What is the one thing that underlies all things?*

  

The standard explanation, as noted in Chapter 1, has been parallel development. Same pressures, same responses, separate trajectories.

  

But the historian who accepts this too quickly should be asked a more precise question: what, exactly, were those pressures? And were they, in fact, as separate as the parallel-development model assumes?

  

  

The collapse was specific. Around 1200 BCE, the Late Bronze Age world — the interconnected civilizations of Egypt, Mycenae, Hatti, and Ugarit, and the Levantine city-states that wove them together — underwent what modern historians describe as the most catastrophic systemic failure in the history of civilization before the modern era. Palace economies collapsed within a generation. The complex trade networks that had carried copper from Cyprus, tin from Anatolia, lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, and grain from Egypt across the entire Eastern Mediterranean — networks that had been operating for centuries and had made possible the extraordinary material richness of the Late Bronze Age courts — dissolved as if they had never existed. Writing systems were lost. In Greece, the Linear B script that had administered the Mycenaean palace economy disappeared so completely that it would not be deciphered until 1952. Cities that had stood for centuries were abandoned or burned.

  

The causes remain disputed: the Sea Peoples, whose identity and origins are still argued over; sustained drought, evidenced in the pollen records and the final desperate letters of the king of Ugarit begging Egypt for grain; internal systems failure, the inevitable fragility of any network so interdependent that a disruption in Cyprus means famine in Athens. Probably all three, and probably more. What is not disputed is the consequence: the continuous world of the Bronze Age — a world organized around divine kingship, palace redistribution economies, and priestly literacies that maintained cosmic order through ritual — was gone.

  

What emerged from the centuries of fragmentation that followed was something entirely new. Smaller, more dispersed communities, stripped of the elaborate administrative and religious machinery of the Bronze Age kingdoms, were forced to find new ways of organizing knowledge, transmitting culture, and — most crucially of all — *understanding the world* without the certainties the Bronze Age had provided.

  

Into this disrupted space came two technologies that would change the structure of human thought itself.

  

The first was the alphabet. Not writing as such — the Bronze Age had writing, complex syllabic and logographic systems maintained by specialist scribes serving palace administrations. But the alphabet was radically different: a small and learnable set of signs that reduced all the sounds of a language to fewer than thirty abstract symbols. What it made possible was something that had never existed before at scale: literacy beyond the scribal class, and — far more consequentially — the externalization of thought. When a person can write an argument down, look at it objectively, correct it, show it to someone else, and argue back against it in writing, something fundamental happens to the mind. It begins to examine itself. The habit of reflection — of treating one's own thoughts as objects to be analyzed — is far harder to develop in an oral culture where every thought vanishes the moment it is spoken.

  

The second technology was coinage. Money had existed before — grain tallies, measured lumps of silver, commodity exchange of formalized kinds. But minted coinage, standardized and stamped with the authority of a state, was philosophically new. It was the first object in human economic life that had no intrinsic value, that derived its meaning entirely from collective agreement, that was purely and entirely *abstract* — a symbol standing for something else, endlessly convertible, belonging to no particular place or person. It trained the communities that used it in abstract equivalence: this object, which has no intrinsic worth, equals that object, which does. This handful of silver represents that jar of wheat. The cognitive habit of abstraction — of looking through the particular to the universal structure underneath — was practiced every time a merchant calculated exchange rates.

  

These two technologies did not cause the philosophical revolutions of the Axial period. But they created the cognitive infrastructure within which those revolutions became possible — training populations in second-order thinking, in reasoning about reasoning, in reflecting on how one acts rather than merely acting. Philosophy, wherever it emerged, emerged in communities that were alphabetically literate and monetized. It is not a coincidence.

  

The Bronze Age had been a world of the continuous cosmos: nature, society, and the divine on a single unbroken plane, maintained by ritual, royal power, and priestly expertise. The collapse shattered this. In its aftermath, for the first time in recorded history, human beings in multiple civilizations simultaneously confronted a question the Bronze Age had not needed to ask: *what is the real shape of things, beneath the surface of the world as it appears?*

  

That question is what produced Socrates and the Buddha within a century of each other.

  

## III. Money, Mind, and the Birth of Philosophy

The economic historian Richard Seaford has proposed the most provocative account of the Axial Age yet offered. His argument, advanced in *Money and the Early Greek Mind* (2004) and developed fully in *The Origins of Philosophy in Ancient Greece and India* (2020), is not about trade routes or diplomatic contact. It is about something far more intimate: the restructuring of human cognition by a new technology.

  

That technology was coinage.

  

In the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, two regions of the world — almost simultaneously — adopted monetized economies based on standardized metal currency. In Ionia, the Lydian kings began minting electrum coins around 600 BCE, and the practice spread rapidly through the Greek city-states of the Aegean. In Northern India, punch-marked silver coins appeared in the same general period, circulating through the emerging urban centers of the *Mahajanapadas* — the "great kingdoms" — of the Gangetic plain.

  

Seaford's argument is simple and devastating: money, as a medium of exchange, is the first genuinely abstract universal in human economic life. It has no intrinsic value. It is not the thing itself — not the wheat, not the goat, not the linen — but a symbol that *represents* the value of all things equally. It is invisible, impersonal, unlimited, and omnipresent. It flows everywhere and belongs to no one. It transforms the qualitatively different into the quantitatively equivalent.

  

What did this new cognitive object train human minds to do?

  

It trained them to look beneath appearances for a hidden universal substance. It trained them to understand that the multiplicity of things we see — the wheat, the goat, the linen, the timber — could all be reduced to a single, abstract, underlying principle that expressed each of their values equivalently. It provided, for the first time in everyday life, a *model* for monism: the philosophical claim that behind the plurality of experience lies one foundational reality.

  

The Milesian philosophers' search for the *arche* — the one substance underlying all things — follows the structure of money's function almost exactly. Water, air, the boundless *apeiron*: these are proposed as the medium that underlies the diversity of appearances the way money underlies the diversity of economic exchange. And in India, the Upanishadic concept of *Brahman* — the single, infinite, impersonal reality in which all phenomenal experience is grounded — performs the same philosophical function. The *Atman*, the individual self that is ultimately identical with Brahman, performs the role of the individual coin: a particular token of a universal, impersonal substance.

  

The parallel is not accidental. It is structural. And it was produced, Seaford argues, by the same material transformation operating on human minds in two different but connected locations — connected precisely by the network of Persian imperial administration, Silk Road trade, and trans-civilizational contact that this chapter is tracing.

  

## IV. The Republic of Reason: Two Political Miracles

Something else happened simultaneously in Ionia and the Gangetic plain in the sixth century BCE, and it is something the standard narrative of the Axial Age rarely pauses to notice.

  

Both regions invented the deliberative republic.

  

In Greece, the *polis* — the city-state — was developing forms of collective governance that were genuinely radical: elected magistrates, citizen assemblies, public debate as the mechanism of decision-making. The Athenian *agora* and the Ionian town squares were physical spaces where the right to argue, to challenge, to contradict authority in public was not merely tolerated but institutionally required. The philosopher and the citizen occupied the same conceptual space. To reason in public was an act of civic participation. The method Socrates would later make famous — the relentless, public, dialectical examination of assumptions — was not an invention of one eccentric mind. It was what the culture of the polis produced when it turned seriously to the question of how to live.

  

In Northern India, almost exactly contemporary with the rise of the Greek polis, a form of governance appeared in the Himalayan foothills that a well-traveled Greek would have recognized immediately: the *Gana-Sangha*. Literally "the assembly of the clan," the Gana-Sangha was an aristocratic republic governed by deliberative councils of noble *Kshatriya* families. These councils — the rajas — debated, voted, and reached decisions by consensus. They admitted no single king with absolute authority. They tolerated unorthodox views. Their assembly halls — *Santhāgaras*, or "halls of truth" — were specifically designed as spaces for deliberation, open to all members of the governing class. In function and in philosophical spirit, they were closer to the Athenian *boule* than to anything the Persian Empire or the monarchies of Egypt had produced.

  

The *Vajji* confederacy — the most famous and powerful of the Gana-Sanghas — was governed by a council of rajas that met in the great city of Vaishali. Vaishali was by any measure one of the most sophisticated urban environments of its era: it had a city wall, parks, a population large enough to sustain luxury commerce, and a reputation for philosophical openness that drew wandering teachers and heterodox thinkers from across the Gangetic plain. The *Shakya* clan republic of Kapilavastu, a smaller Gana-Sangha in the foothills of what is now southern Nepal, operated on the same model. And the Shakya republic was where Siddhartha Gautama was born.

  

Let that detail settle for a moment.

  

The Buddha — whose teachings would become the most widespread religious philosophy in the history of Asia — was not born into a divine kingship or a priestly caste. He was born into a republic. He grew up watching his father and the other clan elders deliberate in assembly. He grew up in a culture that valued argument, consensus, and the public examination of claims. His later method — the dialogue, the probing question, the systematic dismantling of assumptions, the insistence that his followers not accept anything on authority but verify it through their own experience — was not a departure from his cultural inheritance. It was its fullest expression.

  

The Buddhist *Sangha* — the monastic order he founded after his enlightenment — was itself modeled explicitly on the Gana-Sangha. It operated by consensus. It reached decisions through assembly. It gave monks the right to challenge proposed rules and to vote on grievances. A democracy of awakening, structured like the republic where its founder was born.

  

Consider what this means. The Buddha and Socrates — who may have been almost exactly contemporary, both living in the middle decades of the fifth century BCE — were born into political cultures that were, structurally, almost identical: small republican communities governed by deliberative councils, which fostered intellectual cultures that placed a premium on reason, argument, and the public examination of inherited assumptions.

  

Neither Socrates nor the Buddha invented their method from nothing. They inherited it from the politics of their world. And the worlds that produced them — though separated by three thousand miles and everything the conventional map tells us they ought to have been separated by — had arrived, by their own independent paths, at the same political form.

  

That convergence is not nothing. It is evidence.

  

## V. The Philosophers Talk to the Sky

The parallels do not stop at politics or economics. They reach into the deepest recesses of metaphysical inquiry — and here they become almost uncanny.

  

Consider the Nasadiya Sukta — the Hymn of Creation — from the Rigveda, composed sometime between 1200 and 900 BCE, in the ancient priestly Sanskrit of a culture that had been thinking rigorously about cosmological questions for centuries before Greece had a word for philosophy:

  

*Then, there was neither existence nor non-existence. There was no air, no sky beyond it. What covered it? Where was it? What gave it shelter? Was there water, unfathomably deep?*

  

*There was no death then, nor immortality. There was no distinction of night from day. That One breathed windlessly, by its own impulse. Other than that, there was nothing else.*

  

*Who truly knows? Who will here proclaim it? Whence was it born, whence came this creation? The gods came afterwards, with the creation of this universe. Who then knows whence it has arisen? Perhaps it formed itself, or perhaps it did not — the Overseer in the highest heaven, he only knows, or perhaps he does not know.*

  

Read those last lines again. *Perhaps even the gods do not know.*

  

This poem was composed, at the latest, around 900 BCE — roughly three hundred years before Thales of Miletus asked his famous question about the nature of the *arche*. And yet it asks, in remarkable anticipation, the same question: what was there before? What is the one thing from which all things came? And it adds a turn of epistemological humility that the Milesian tradition itself never quite produced: the acknowledgment that the question may be finally unanswerable — that even the divine standpoint does not guarantee access to origins.

  

Now read Anaximander: *The apeiron — the indefinite — is the first principle and element of all existing things. It is neither water nor any other element, but a different nature, which is boundless. From it comes the birth of all worlds. Into it they dissolve, as is ordained of necessity.*

  

The *apeiron* is boundless, indefinite, and foundational — philosophically identical in function to the *Brahman* of the Upanishads, the single reality underlying the differentiated appearances of the world.

  

Or Heraclitus, who was born to a priestly family in Ephesus, who was famously contemptuous of the crowd and of conventional wisdom, and who left behind fragments that have troubled translators for two and a half millennia: *All things are one. The Logos is universal. Most people act as though they had understanding all their own.* Behind the surface flux of things — the fire, the river, the day-and-night — Heraclitus saw a single rational principle that ordered all transformation. The world was not chaos. It was a unity too deep for ordinary perception to reach.

  

The Indian tradition had already named that unity. The *Rigveda*'s *Rta* — cosmic order, the principle behind the regularity of seasons and the reliability of dawn — was the Vedic precursor to what the Upanishads would formalize as *Brahman*. The *Dharma* of the early Buddhist tradition — not merely moral law but the fundamental structure of the way things are — performed the same philosophical function as the *Logos*: the deep pattern that ordinary minds miss because they are too busy managing the surface of experience to see what underlies it.

  

Consider one more parallel, perhaps the most extraordinary of all.

  

In the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*, the sage Yajnavalkya is pressed repeatedly by his student to define the ultimate nature of the Self. Each definition he offers, his student accepts — and presses further. Finally, Yajnavalkya abandons positive description altogether and responds with a method the West would not independently formulate for another three centuries: *neti, neti* — "not this, not this." The absolute cannot be grasped by any positive attribute. Every description is already a limitation. The ultimate reality is defined only by what it is not, reached only by the systematic removal of everything it cannot be.

  

This is apophasis. This is the *via negativa*, the path of negation, which Meister Eckhart would apply to Christian mysticism in the fourteenth century and which pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite had already applied to Christian theology in the fifth. It appears in Indian philosophy, fully formed and rigorously applied, in a text that predates Plato by at least a century.

  

And one more, which deserves more attention than it typically receives: the Charvaka school — the materialist, skeptical, anti-priestly tradition of ancient India that emerged roughly contemporary with the Milesian philosophers — proposed an account of reality based on atoms and void as the fundamental constituents. Leucippus and Democritus, the Greek philosophers credited with inventing atomic theory, did so in the late fifth century BCE. The Charvaka tradition precedes them.

  

## VI. The Question the Network Raises

At this point, a careful reader will want to pause and ask: is this contact, or is it coincidence?

  

It is a fair question, and it deserves an honest answer: we cannot prove direct contact for most of these philosophical parallels. The surviving evidence does not give us a moment where Thales and an Upanishadic sage compared notes on the *arche* and *Brahman*. The Persepolis tablets tell us Greeks and Indians worked side by side — but they do not record what they talked about during the hours when they were not being counted by the imperial census-taker.

  

But this framing — contact versus coincidence — may itself be a false binary. It imports the assumption of the divided world that this book is dismantling. It asks us to choose between two possibilities: either these ideas traveled in a straight line from one mind to another, or they arose completely independently. But there is a third possibility, and it is the one the evidence most strongly supports.

  

Call it *resonance within a shared field.*

  

The Achaemenid Empire did not need to organize philosophical seminars between Greeks and Indians for ideas to begin moving between them. It needed only to do what it already did: create the conditions for sustained, multi-generational, trans-civilizational contact. It needed traders who moved between Ionian ports and Indian satraps. It needed administrators who spoke Aramaic in both directions. It needed military units that mixed Greek mercenaries with Indian infantry and Persian cavalry in the same campaigns. It needed, in short, a world — and it created one.

  

Within that world, ideas move the way water moves through porous stone: not in a straight line from source to destination, but through percolation, through the slow saturation of a medium. A merchant from Miletus who has spent time in a Bactrian trading post brings home not a Buddhist treatise — which doesn't exist yet in written form — but a set of questions: about impermanence, about the nature of the self, about what lies behind appearances, that he heard argued in a marketplace he can barely describe to his neighbors. A Persian official who has served in both Susa and Taxila carries in his memory not a philosophical system but a sensibility: that the world is more complicated than the myths say, that there are people in the east who have thought very hard about questions that the west hasn't fully asked yet, and that perhaps the questioning itself is the point.

  

Ideas travel in smoke before they travel in books. They travel in the half-remembered arguments of travelers, in the borrowed metaphors of diplomats, in the questions that one culture absorbs from another without knowing that it is absorbing anything at all.

  

The Hidden Network was not a school. It was a weather system.

  

What that weather system was carrying, it is worth pausing to say precisely. The Greek and Indian philosophical traditions, for all their remarkable parallels, were not simply saying the same things in different languages. They were developing different dimensions of a common human wisdom — and each had, over their separate centuries of reflection, developed what the other most needed.

  

The Greeks were astonishingly good at one thing: the theoretical examination of the world, the systematic search for principles, the discipline of reasoning that could be argued, tested, and refined by others. What Aristotle called *sophia* — and what the pre-Socratic tradition was pioneering — was the capacity to extract universal knowledge from particular observation, to ascend from appearances to structure. The pre-Socratics were the first people in the Western tradition to insist that the world had an intelligible shape that reason could reach — not through myth, not through revelation, but through systematic argument.

  

The Indian traditions were developing something different: something that the Greek tradition had barely begun to name. It was not *sophia* — theoretical knowledge of structures. It was not quite *phronesis* either — the practical wisdom of knowing how to act well in particular circumstances. It was something for which Greek had no fully adequate term at all: the knowledge that comes not from argument but from transformation, the understanding that changes not just what you know but who you are. The Upanishadic sages and the early Buddhist teachers were not primarily claiming that the universe had this or that ultimate structure. They were developing a *practice* — a set of disciplines for the whole human being — that would allow the practitioner to know reality from the inside rather than from the outside.

  

Socrates said that wisdom begins in wonder. But Plato and Aristotle had different accounts of what wonder was for. For Aristotle, wonder generates a question, the question gets answered, and wonder can rest — resolved into knowledge. For Plato, wonder does not resolve. It deepens. It pulls the philosopher toward a kind of awe that does not settle into answers but continues to transform the person who sustains it. The Indian tradition, in its deepest registers, was doing something closer to the Platonic account: wonder sustained, wonder turned into a discipline of attention, wonder that ends not in a theory but in a transformed life.

  

Each tradition had developed what the other most needed. And the Hidden Network — Persian roads, Aramaic-speaking merchants, multilingual diplomats, the vast slow machinery of the empire's trans-civilizational contact — was the medium through which, slowly and incompletely and invisibly, they were already beginning to find each other.

  

## VII. What Was Already in Motion Before Alexander Arrived

By the time Alexander the Great crossed the Hellespont in 334 BCE and began the campaign that would carry him to the banks of the Indus, the world he was entering was not virgin philosophical territory.

  

The Upanishadic tradition was already centuries old — its texts debated, glossed, and transmitted through teacher-student lineages that stretched back to the forest academies of the Vedic age. The Buddha had already lived, taught, and died — around 483 BCE by most scholarly reckonings, more than a century and a half before Alexander's legions crossed the Hindu Kush. The first Buddhist councils had already taken place. The Sangha was already a functioning international institution, with monasteries stretching from the Ganges plain to the edges of the Achaemenid world. The Jains had already articulated the doctrine of *anekantavada* — the plurality of perspectives, the impossibility of any single viewpoint capturing the whole truth — that would have struck any Greek Skeptic as immediately resonant. The Ajivika school, now largely forgotten, was already teaching a doctrine of radical determinism — that all things unfold according to a fixed cosmic pattern, that human will neither accelerates nor retards one's spiritual development — that would have found a ready audience among the Stoics who came after.

  

And on the Greek side: Heraclitus had already written his fragments on the Logos. Parmenides had already argued that change is an illusion and being is one — a claim so structurally similar to the Upanishadic doctrine of the unity of *Brahman* that the scholar Thomas McEvilley spent two hundred pages of *The Shape of Ancient Thought* carefully mapping the correspondence. Plato's *Republic* — the most ambitious attempt in Greek thought to imagine a society governed by wisdom rather than power — had already been composed. The Academy was already training philosophers. The Skeptical tradition, whose deep affinities with Indian epistemological thought we will trace in Chapter 7, was already beginning to take shape in the questioning of Pyrrho's predecessors.

  

When Alexander's philosophers encountered the gymnosophists on the banks of the Indus in 327 BCE, they were not encountering an alien tradition for the first time. They were encountering a tradition that had been — through the slow percolation of the Hidden Network, through the machinery of the empire, through the long traffic of merchants and diplomats and multilingual administrators along the Persian royal roads — already partially familiar. Already resonant. Already, in the deepest sense, not entirely *other*.

  

The meeting that appears in the histories as a collision of worlds was, in reality, a recognition.

  

Alexander's campaign did not introduce East and West to each other. It accelerated a relationship that was already underway. It widened the channel of a river that had been flowing, slow and deep, for three centuries. The drama of his conquest — the battles, the sieges, the marriages, the death in Babylon — is real and important. But the intellectual history of the Greek-Indian encounter begins not with Alexander's spear but with the tablets in Persepolis, with the ships of Scylax on the Indus, with the punch-marked coins of the Ganges plain and the electrum staters of Lydia, with every anonymous merchant and multilingual bureaucrat and wandering teacher who had been carrying questions across the corridor of the ancient world since before either of the traditions they connected had found its name.

  

Two rivers that had been running parallel for centuries finally reached the same valley — and discovered that they had been drawing from the same source all along.
