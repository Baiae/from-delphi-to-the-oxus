# Chapter 10 — An Army of Greek Monks

  

*"At that time, Yona Mahadhammarakhita, a thera of the city of Alasanda, led thirty thousand monks."* — *Mahavamsa* (The Great Chronicle), Chapter 29

  

## I. The March from Alexandria

Around 130 BCE, a column of monks set out from a Greek city in the Hindu Kush and walked to Sri Lanka.

  

The city was Alexandria of the Caucasus — one of the chain of Alexandrias that the Macedonian conqueror had planted across his empire like markers on a road he intended to return to and never did. It stood near modern Kabul, where the Panjshir and Ghorband rivers meet before descending toward the Indus plain: a place of ancient strategic importance, a node on the routes connecting India to Central Asia to the Mediterranean world. Alexander had founded it; his successors had built it into one of the principal cities of the Greco-Bactrian kingdom. By 130 BCE it was a Greek city in the Indo-Greek world, governed by Indo-Greek kings who minted Greek coins and patronized both Greek gymnasium culture and Buddhist monasteries.

  

The monks who left Alexandria did not march to a battle. They walked — the distance is approximately three thousand miles — to the island of Lanka, at the southern tip of the subcontinent, to attend the consecration of a stupa.

  

The stupa was the Ruwanwelisaya. It stood — it still stands, rebuilt and restored, rising white against the sky of Anuradhapura — as the monument of King Dutugamunu, who had unified Lanka and who built his great stupa as the culminating act of a reign he understood as religious as much as political. He wanted the world's Buddhists there. He sent invitations. They came.

  

From the *Mahavamsa*, the Great Chronicle of Sri Lanka, Chapter 29:

  

*At that time, Yona Mahadhammarakhita, a thera of the city of Alasanda, led thirty thousand monks.*

  

*Yona.* The Pali word for Greek.

  

## II. What It Meant to Be a Monk

Before the number thirty thousand can land with its proper weight, we need to understand what the word *monk* meant in the Buddhist world of the second century BCE.

  

It did not mean, as it might in a loose modern usage, someone privately devoted to religious practice. It meant something precise, institutional, and demanding. A Buddhist monk — a member of the *sangha*, the monastic community — had been formally ordained in a ceremony requiring a quorum of existing fully ordained monks, had recited the precepts before that assembly, had received the robe and the bowl that constituted, along with a razor, a needle, a water strainer, a belt, and a robe-cloth, the eight possessions he was now permitted. He owned nothing else. He ate only what was given. He did not cook, buy, or store food. He did not touch money. He did not sleep under the same roof as a woman. He shaved his head. He walked, wherever he went.

  

The *Vinaya* — the monastic code, the vast body of regulations governing every aspect of monastic life — specified the rules with a precision that could fairly be called obsessive, and that reflected a profound institutional understanding: a community of people who own nothing and depend entirely on the generosity of laypeople for survival has to be trusted. Its members have to be, and have to be seen to be, people of absolute reliability. The rules existed to make the sangha the kind of institution that a merchant, a farmer, a king, could trust with a meal and with their children and with the questions that kept them awake at night.

  

This was not a casual identity. This was not a label you adopted because you found a tradition spiritually interesting. This was the *sangha* — the third of the Three Jewels, alongside the Buddha and the Dharma — and membership in it was the most serious institutional commitment the Buddhist world recognized.

  

The thirty thousand monks of Mahadhammarakhita were not casual sympathizers. They were the real thing. They had taken the vows. They wore the robes. They had renounced the world in the full, formal, legally recognized sense that the Buddhist world of the second century BCE understood.

  

And they were Greek.

  

## III. Ashoka's Greeks

To understand how this was possible — how a Greek city in Afghanistan had produced not one or two Buddhist monks but a community large enough to send thirty thousand of its members on pilgrimage — we need to go back a century.

  

In the middle of the third century BCE, the Mauryan Emperor Ashoka — the man who had converted to Buddhism after the slaughter at Kalinga and spent the rest of his reign trying to build a civilization worthy of the Dharma — organized missions to carry Buddhism beyond the boundaries of his empire. He inscribed the record of this project on rocks and pillars across the subcontinent, in proclamations that survive to this day. And one of the missions he sent — inscribed in his Rock Edict XIII, among the most remarkable documents of the ancient world — was to the courts of the Greek kings.

  

By name. Antiochus II of Syria. Ptolemy III of Egypt. Antigonus of Macedonia. Magas of Cyrene. Alexander of Epirus. Ashoka sent emissaries to these kings. He listed them by name on a rock.

  

But he also sent a Greek to his own western frontier. Rock Edict V names a Greek monk called *Dhammarakhita* — Dharma-Guardian — as one of the key figures in Ashoka's missionary program. This is not the same Dhammarakhita who would become Nāgasena's teacher in the following century: the name appears to have been borne by a line of distinguished Greek Buddhist monks, a kind of title within the Greek-Buddhist community as much as a personal name. But the pattern is the same. A Greek, bearing a Sanskrit Buddhist name, carrying the Dharma westward into the regions where Greeks lived.

  

Ashoka's missions worked. By the time of Menander's reign, a century later, the *Yona* — the Greeks — were a recognized and fully integrated category within the Buddhist world. Inscriptions name Greek donors. The *Mahavamsa* records Greek monks. The coins show the Dharmachakra alongside Athena. And the city of Alexandria, founded by a Greek conqueror who never heard of Buddhism, had become a city with enough Buddhist monasteries and enough ordained monks to send thirty thousand of them to Lanka.

  

## IV. The Evidence Beyond the Chronicle

The *Mahavamsa* is the principal source for the march of the Yona monks, and it deserves to be treated with the care appropriate to a text composed several centuries after the events it describes. Ancient chronicles use large numbers for ceremonial effect, to convey significance rather than literal precision. Thirty thousand may be conventional shorthand for *a vast number*. It would not be the first time a chronicle inflated the count of an army or a delegation.

  

But even heavily discounted, the core claim is extraordinary. A Greek Buddhist senior monk — a *thera*, meaning he had been fully ordained for at minimum ten years, meaning the Greek-Buddhist community he came from had been running long enough to produce that kind of seniority — led a significant delegation from a Greek city to the most important Buddhist ceremony of the era. That is the historical minimum of what the *Mahavamsa* claims. And it is corroborated from outside the chronicle tradition.

  

The Greek meridarch — civil governor of a province — named Theodorus left an inscription, found on a vase inside a stupa, recording that he enshrined relics of the Buddha. It is in Kharoshthi script — meaning Theodorus was literate in Indian scripts, which is itself significant — and dated to the reign of Menander or one of his successors. A civil administrator, a Greek official, handling Buddha relics and recording the act formally for posterity. The inscription's language is Hellenistic civic boilerplate — *for the welfare of the mass of the people* — the standard formula Greek officials used when dedicating public monuments. Transplanted here, intact, into a Buddhist votive context. Theodorus did not find the phrase incongruous. Neither language, Buddhist or Greek, needed to be translated. They ran together in the inscription as naturally as they ran together, apparently, in his mind.

  

Buddhist manuscripts written in cursive Greek have been found in Afghanistan — texts praising various Buddhas and including references to Mahayana Buddhist concepts, written in the Greek alphabet by people who were clearly comfortable with both Greek writing and Buddhist doctrine. The Greek-alphabet representation of a Buddhist text includes the transliterated phrase *λωγοασφαροραζοβοδδο* — a phonetic rendering of a Sanskrit Buddhist formula that only a fully literate Buddhist could have produced. Someone sat down with a Greek pen and a Buddhist text and made them speak the same language.

  

The coinage of the Indo-Greek kings, considered as a body, shows the gradual interpenetration of the two traditions: some coins bear Greek deities exclusively, some bear the Dharmachakra exclusively, some bear both simultaneously. The same royal mint, the same decade, both traditions — suggesting not an ideological tension being navigated but something simpler: that these were not experienced as separate. That the community that produced these coins lived, apparently without great difficulty, in both worlds at once.

  

## V. What It Took to Get There

Three thousand miles from Kabul to Anuradhapura.

  

The monks would have walked the high passes of the Hindu Kush in late spring, when the snows were still retreating from the heights. They would have descended to the Indus, crossed it, and turned south through the Punjab — the land of five rivers, the land that Alexander had entered with such excitement and such difficulty, the land where the Indo-Greek kingdoms were thickest. They would have passed through Taxila, the ancient university city that had been a center of learning for centuries before Alexander arrived and centuries after. They would have crossed the Gangetic plain, perhaps stopping at the great monasteries of the Magadha region. And then the long passage south, through territory that became less familiar as the landscape changed — the Deccan plateau, the forests of the south, finally the narrow Palk Strait and the crossing to Lanka.

  

The *Mahavamsa* does not give us the route. It gives us the arrival, which it describes with the ceremonial language appropriate to a moment of cosmic significance: the monks came, the earth shook in six ways, the flowers fell.

  

But the arrival was real. The stupa was real. The ceremony was real. And the monks — Greek monks from Afghanistan, walking to Lanka for the consecration of a monument to the liberation of all beings — were real.

  

## VI. Mahadhammarakhita

We do not know much about the man who led them beyond what his name tells us and what the *Mahavamsa* records. But both are eloquent.

  

The *Mahavamsa* specifies his rank: *thera*. This is not a courtesy title. It is a formal designation within the Buddhist monastic hierarchy, requiring a minimum of ten years of full ordination in good standing. To be a *thera* in 130 BCE meant that Mahadhammarakhita had been a fully ordained monk since at least 140 BCE — which means, in turn, that the Greek-Buddhist community of Alexandria had been producing and sustaining ordained monastics for a generation. The institution was old enough to have elders.

  

His name is Sanskrit Buddhist — the ordination name given to him when he entered the sangha, replacing whatever Greek name he was born with. That name we do not know. It has been replaced by the name he chose, or was given, when he committed himself to the path. *Maha* — great. *Dhamma* — the teaching. *Rakhita* — guardian. He was, in the most literal possible sense, the Great Protector of the Dharma.

  

The replacement is not erasure. It is transformation. The Greek person who became Mahadhammarakhita did not stop being Greek in any simple sense — his origin was Greek, his cultural formation was Greek, the language he was born into was Greek. But he had found a path that transcended those origins, or rather that contained them within something larger. The Greek analytical tradition that had shaped his mind — the same tradition that had shaped Dhammarakhita before him, and through Dhammarakhita had shaped Nāgasena, and through Nāgasena had shaped Menander — was now in the service of the Dharma. It was inseparable from it.

  

He is, in this way, the perfect emblem of what Greco-Bactria had produced. Not a Greek who had abandoned his tradition for an Indian one. Not an Indian who had been Hellenized by force or by fashion. But a human being in whom two great traditions had met, and in meeting, had produced something that neither tradition alone could have produced.

  

Something that marched thirty thousand strong to the southern tip of the subcontinent to celebrate the consecration of a monument to the liberation of all beings.

  

## VII. The Refutation

This chapter exists to make a single argument with the force it deserves: the identification of Buddhism as an "Eastern" or "Asian" religion — as something that belongs, by cultural right and historical destiny, to the peoples of East and South Asia, and not to the peoples of European descent — is a modern invention with no ancient authority.

  

It is not a fact of history. It is a projection of nineteenth-century racial and cultural categories onto a world that did not organize itself along those lines.

  

The Greeks who marched to Sri Lanka in the second century BCE did not experience Buddhism as a foreign religion they were borrowing from a superior culture. They experienced it as their religion — the tradition in which they had taken vows, the community to which they belonged, the path they were walking toward liberation. The *Mahavamsa* does not record them as curious observers or respectful guests. It records them as participants. As monks. As people whose presence at the stupa dedication mattered, whose spiritual standing was recognized, whose community was real.

  

The Theodorus inscription makes the same point from a different angle. Theodorus was not a monk. He was a civil administrator — a *meridarch*, a governor. He was managing provinces on behalf of an Indo-Greek king. And when he came to perform a religious act, a private act of Buddhist devotion, he did what Greek officials had always done when performing acts of public religious significance: he recorded it formally, in the local script, for the welfare of the people. Buddhist content, Greek form, seamlessly integrated. Not a synthesis requiring negotiation. Simply what the man did.

  

Buddhism has been cosmopolitan from its inception. The Buddha himself — who taught that the Dharma was available to all beings regardless of caste, birth, or social position — would not have been surprised by a Greek monk. He had already disrupted the assumption that spiritual liberation was the exclusive province of any particular group. What the Greeks of Bactria and the Indo-Greek kingdoms were doing in the second century BCE was simply taking that teaching at its word.

  

The surprise — the thing that history has needed to recover and that the conventional narrative has consistently suppressed — is not that Greeks became Buddhists. The surprise is that this is surprising. That we have allowed ourselves, for so long, to imagine a world in which the traditions that shaped Western civilization and the traditions that shaped Eastern civilization were separate, parallel, non-communicating streams — when the evidence, in inscription and coin and chronicle and philosophical text, consistently shows something else entirely.

  

## VIII. The Implication

Chapter 9 recorded the philosophical transformation at individual scale: Menander, the most sophisticated king in the Indo-Greek world, encountering in Nāgasena a wisdom that his own tradition had been circling for centuries, and turning toward it. That chapter is about one mind being changed.

  

This chapter is about institutions. About what happens when philosophical transformation scales from a king's audience chamber to a city's monasteries, from a single encounter to a community's way of life. The thirty thousand monks — whatever the literal precision of that number — are the institutional confirmation of what the *Milindapañha* records at the level of individual encounter. This was not unusual. This was normal. The Greeks of the Indo-Greek world had been doing this for generations by the time Mahadhammarakhita led his column south. The sangha of Greek-Buddhist monks was large enough to send a delegation of this scale because it had been building itself, monk by monk, ordination by ordination, for more than a century.

  

And it was building something else, too — something that would outlast the Indo-Greek kingdoms, outlast the Greek language in Bactria, outlast everything that the Macedonian conquest had planted in the east. It was building the tradition that would carve the first human face of the Buddha. It was building the visual vocabulary that would travel the Silk Road and transform the art of Asia. It was building the bridge over which, centuries later, a monk named Lokaksema would carry the first Mahayana sutras into the Chinese language.

  

The march to Lanka was not an ending. It was evidence that something had been built strong enough to go anywhere.

  

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

***Mahavamsa*** **(The Great Chronicle)**, Chapter 29 (c. 4th–5th century CE; Pali). The record of the thirty thousand Yona monks and Mahadhammarakhita. Standard English translation: Wilhelm Geiger, *The Mahavamsa, or the Great Chronicle of Ceylon* (Pali Text Society, 1912).

  

**Ashokan Rock Edicts**: Rock Edict XIII (Greek kings named by Ashoka as targets of his Buddhist missions); Rock Edict V (Dhammarakhita named as a missionary to the western regions). The standard scholarly edition is N.A. Nikam and Richard McKeon, *The Edicts of Asoka* (University of Chicago Press, 1959).

  

**Theodorus Meridarch Inscription** (1st century BCE; Kharoshthi script). The Greek civil governor enshrining Buddha relics. Published in Konow, S., *Kharoshthi Inscriptions* (Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. II.1, 1929).

### Modern Scholarly Works

**Tarn, W.W.** ***The Greeks in Bactria and India*****.** Chapter X: "The Greeks and Buddhism." The foundational scholarly account of Greek participation in Buddhism.

  

**McEvilley, Thomas.** ***The Shape of Ancient Thought*****.** Chapter 20: on the scale and institutional depth of Greek Buddhist participation; the Theodorus inscription analyzed in detail.

  

**Keown, Damien.** ***Buddhism: A Very Short Introduction*****. Oxford University Press, 1996.** Context for the institutional structure of the Buddhist sangha and what full participation in it meant.

  

**Neelis, Jason.** ***Early Buddhist Transmission and Trade Networks*****. Brill, 2011.** The mechanics of Buddhist transmission along Silk Road routes; the monastery as a node in the network.
