# Chapter 15 — Buddhists in Alexandria

  

*"A Buddhist gravestone decorated with the dharma wheel has been found in Ptolemaic Alexandria, proving that Buddhists lived in the Hellenistic Mediterranean."* — Archaeological evidence, Ptolemaic period

  

## I. The City That Held Everything

Alexandria, Egypt. The most cosmopolitan city in the ancient world.

  

In the second and first centuries BCE, Alexandria was the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean: home to the greatest library in the ancient world, to the *Mouseion* — the research institute that gave us the word "museum" — to the mathematical genius of Euclid and Eratosthenes, to the astronomical precision of Hipparchus, to the medical advances of Herophilus and Erasistratus. It was also the commercial capital of the eastern Mediterranean, the entrepôt through which the goods of the Indian Ocean trade flowed into the Mediterranean world: spices, silks, ivory, cotton, aromatics, gemstones.

  

And with the goods came people. And with the people came ideas.

  

The mechanism of this exchange was specific and well-documented. Every year, when the monsoon winds reversed direction, ships from the ports of western India — from Barygaza and Muzaris and the ports of the Malabar coast — loaded their cargoes of pepper and muslin and ivory and set sail for the Red Sea. They made landfall at Berenice, the Roman port on the Egyptian Red Sea coast, from which goods and passengers traveled overland or by canal to Alexandria. The scale of this traffic, by the first century CE, was enormous: the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a merchant's guide to the Indian Ocean trade written in that period, describes the routes in meticulous detail and implies a volume of commerce — hundreds of ships per year, in the Roman period — that was transforming the economy of the Mediterranean world.

  

The harbor of Alexandria received not just Indian goods but Indian people. Merchants. Traders. Agents. And, traveling among them, the wandering teachers of the Indian śramaṇa tradition — the monks and philosophers who were, by the first century BCE, the most mobile professional class in the ancient world, trained to wander, accustomed to long journeys, carrying nothing but their robes and their teaching and the institution of the Buddhist sangha, which meant that wherever they stopped, there was community.

  

In this environment — in the city where this traffic converged, where the goods and the people and the ideas of the Indian Ocean world met the intellectual apparatus of the Hellenistic and then Roman Mediterranean — we should expect to find traces of Indian thought and practice. And we do.

  

## II. The Dharma Wheel in Egypt

The most striking piece of physical evidence is also the most concrete: a gravestone decorated with the dharma wheel — the *dharmachakra*, the eight-spoked wheel that is one of the oldest and most fundamental symbols of Buddhism — found in excavations at Ptolemaic Alexandria.

  

A Buddhist gravestone. In Egypt. In the Ptolemaic period — before the Roman conquest of Egypt in 30 BCE, in the height of the Hellenistic world.

  

This means that at least one person in Alexandria — possibly many more — was Buddhist enough to be buried with Buddhist symbolism, in a city where Buddhist practice was unusual enough that the gravestone is remarkable, but present enough that someone knew what to inscribe and had reason to inscribe it.

  

It is a small object. Its implications are not small. It demonstrates that Buddhist individuals were living and dying in Alexandria during the Hellenistic period — not just passing through, not just stopping for trade, but settling, long enough to be buried, in the intellectual and commercial capital of the western world.

  

To this evidence, recent archaeology has added something even more striking. In 2022, excavations at Berenice Troglodytica — the ancient Red Sea port through which Indian goods flowed northward to Alexandria — uncovered a Buddha statue in a temple precinct dedicated to the Egyptian gods Isis and Serapis. The statue stood alongside figures of Hindu deities. The temple itself speaks to the religious eclecticism of a port city that received worshippers from every corner of the Indian Ocean world; the Buddha's presence among the temple's images suggests that Buddhist veneration was not merely tolerated in these spaces but actively practiced — that the port community included people for whom the Buddha was an object of devotion in the same sense that Isis was an object of devotion for the Egyptians.

  

From the gravestone of an unknown Buddhist in Ptolemaic Alexandria to a Buddha statue in an Egyptian temple of Isis: the evidence spans several centuries, and it points in the same direction. Buddhists were not merely passing through. They were here.

  

## III. The Mission That Reached the Kings

The physical evidence confirms a presence. But the presence had a history — a history that begins not with anonymous merchants and travelers but with the most ambitious act of religious diplomacy in the ancient world.

  

In the 250s BCE, the Mauryan emperor Ashoka — who had embraced Buddhism after the horror of the Kalinga war and had made the propagation of the dharma the central project of his reign — inscribed a rock edict at Shahbazgarhi in what is now Pakistan. The Thirteenth Rock Edict is a remarkable document in many ways. But one of its most remarkable features is a list of names.

  

Ashoka records that he has sent teachers of the dharma to the kings of the Greek world: to Antiochus II Theos, whose Seleucid empire stretched from Syria to the borders of Bactria; to Ptolemy II Philadelphus, king of Egypt, in whose capital Alexandria was being built into the wonder of the Mediterranean world; to Antigonos Gonatas, king of Macedonia; to Magas of Cyrene; to Alexander II of Epirus. He names them specifically, as recipients of a deliberate outreach — an Indian emperor, in the middle of the third century BCE, dispatching Buddhist teachers to the courts of the Mediterranean.

  

We do not know how far these missions penetrated. The Seleucid court, at least, was accessible: Ashoka's empire bordered the Seleucid territories in what is now Afghanistan, and Buddhist missionaries were already active in the Seleucid-controlled regions. Whether teachers reached Alexandria, reached the court of Ptolemy II, received a proper welcome rather than polite incomprehension — this we cannot say. The Greek-speaking world left no record of specifically acknowledging Ashoka's embassy.

  

But the initiative was real. The dharma was being actively carried westward, by imperial decree, to the most powerful rulers of the Mediterranean world. The fact that no surviving Greek text records the reception of Ashoka's missionaries tells us something about Greek record-keeping — about what the Greeks considered worth writing down — but it does not tell us that the missionaries did not come.

  

## IV. The Indian Who Died in Athens

Around 20 BCE, an Indian teacher named Zarmanochegas — "the leader of the śramaṇas" — arrived in Athens.

  

He had traveled from India to the court of Caesar Augustus as part of an Indian diplomatic delegation, the kind of embassy that the *Periplus* and other sources record as a regular feature of the Indo-Roman relationship. He reached Athens. And there, in one of the most astonishing public acts in the ancient world, he announced that he intended to die.

  

The Greeks understood what he was doing — or thought they did. The gymnosophist tradition of voluntary death, the deliberate choice of the moment of departure from life, was part of the image of Indian philosophy that had circulated in the Greek world since Onesicritus's accounts of the naked sages six hundred years earlier. Calanus, the Indian philosopher who had accompanied Alexander, had burned himself on a funeral pyre at Susa in 323 BCE, cheerfully, with Alexander watching, having declared that he preferred this to the decline of disease. The story was famous. It was the most vivid illustration the Greek world had of what it meant to take the Indian philosophical tradition seriously all the way to the end.

  

Zarmanochegas — described in some sources as Zarmarus — was doing the same. He gave away his possessions. He said his farewells. He was cheerful and composed. He climbed onto the pyre he had prepared. And he burned.

  

Caesar Augustus gave him an elaborate tomb. Athenians pointed it out to visitors for the next century. Plutarch knew the story. Strabo recorded that Zarmanochegas left a Greek inscription on his monument: an Indian from the port city of Barygaza, having lived according to the customs of his fathers, departing in this way. The monument was still known in the first century CE as "the Indian's Monument."

  

An Indian śramaṇa, dying in Athens, remembered in the city of Plato and Aristotle for a century after his death. Not as an exotic curiosity — as a philosopher, someone whose way of life and whose death expressed a coherent account of what the human life was for.

  

The Indian philosophical tradition was not a rumor in the Mediterranean world. It was present, in flesh and bone, dying in public in the most famous city of the ancient world.

  

## V. The Therapeutae

The most intriguing evidence for an active Buddhist presence in Alexandria is neither archaeological nor epigraphic. It is institutional.

  

In the first century CE, the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria wrote a treatise he called *De Vita Contemplativa* — On the Contemplative Life — describing a community that lived near Lake Mareotis, a few miles southwest of Alexandria. He called them the Therapeutae: "healers" or, perhaps, "servants of God." And what he described was extraordinary.

  

The Therapeutae had renounced property. They had renounced family obligations. They lived in individual dwellings — Philo called each one a *monastērion*, a word that gave the Latin-speaking world its term for "monastery" — arranged in a loose community outside the city. During the week, they remained in their individual cells, studying, contemplating, composing hymns and treatises. They met on the seventh day for communal assembly; they met more elaborately for the communal feast.

  

The feast was conducted according to an elaborate protocol: senior members reclined, junior members served standing, no wine was consumed until the philosophical discourse was complete. Both men and women were members of the community. They fasted extensively. They practiced what Philo describes as a rigorous interior discipline — the quieting of the passions, the cultivation of a settled, undisturbed awareness — that was the prerequisite for the contemplative encounter with the divine.

  

Philo claimed they were Jewish. But the practices he describes had no direct precedent in the Jewish tradition of the period. The Essenes, the other great Jewish contemplative community, practiced communal poverty and celibacy — but without the full communal renunciation of family property, without the individual cells for solitary practice, without the elaborate hierarchy of seniority. The Therapeutae's institutional structure, their integration of women, their combination of individual hermitic practice with communal assembly, their emphasis on formal study and teaching as modes of spiritual development — all of this maps far more precisely onto Buddhist monastic organization than onto anything in the Jewish tradition.

  

The word *Therapeutae* is itself suggestive. Philo's etymologies — "healers" or "servants" — are plausible in Greek. But some scholars have noted that *Theravada* — the name of the oldest surviving school of Buddhist monasticism, the school that sent Mahadhammarakhita and his thirty thousand monks to Sri Lanka — means "the teaching of the elders." Is *Therapeutae* a Hellenized rendering of *Theravada*, adapted through the semantic field of service and healing? The etymology is contested and the consensus uncertain. But the question it raises — whether this Alexandrian community was a Western adaptation of Buddhist institutional practice, grafted onto a Jewish religious framework — is not a question that can be answered simply by pointing to the absence of proof. The evidence has been present in Philo's text for two thousand years. What it means remains genuinely open.

  

## VI. The Philosophical Atmosphere

The Alexandrian intellectual world of the second and first centuries BCE was one in which the boundaries between philosophical schools were more porous than ancient school histories suggest.

  

The great philosophical traditions of the Hellenistic world — Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism, Neoplatonism — were all present in Alexandria, all reading each other's texts, all responding to each other's arguments. The Library contained works from every tradition that the Hellenistic world knew about. The Alexandrian habit of comparative reading — of finding structural parallels between disparate traditions, of testing the insights of one tradition against the resources of another — was the intellectual water in which everyone in the city swam.

  

In this environment, exposure to Indian philosophical ideas would not have been inert. It entered the atmosphere. It influenced what questions were asked, what metaphors were available, what solutions to perennial problems seemed plausible. The gymnosophists were in the philosophical literature. Ashoka's missionaries had, at least officially, been dispatched to the city's ruling dynasty. Indian goods and Indian people were arriving every sailing season at the harbor. And — as the gravestone and the Berenice Buddha and Philo's Therapeutae all suggest — some of those people brought their practices with them and maintained them in their new home.

  

No single piece of evidence is decisive. Each can be explained away. The gravestone: perhaps an isolated individual. Zarmanochegas: a curiosity, admired from a distance. The Therapeutae: perhaps genuinely a Jewish sect with parallel practices. The Berenice Buddha: perhaps a trade object, not a cult image.

  

But the evidence is cumulative. And cumulative evidence has a weight that individual pieces do not. By the end of the Hellenistic period, Alexandria was a city in which Buddhist practices had left physical traces, in which an active contemplative community bore a structural resemblance to Buddhist monasticism, in which Indian philosophy was part of the intellectual furniture of educated people. The question of whether this constitutes "influence" in any philosophically meaningful sense is a question about what we mean by influence — whether we mean only direct textual transmission, or whether we include the subtler permeation of ideas into an atmosphere that shapes what seems possible, what seems already half-said, what feels like recognition rather than discovery.

  

The Alexandrian intellectual world, in the two centuries around the turn of the Common Era, was an atmosphere. And Indian philosophy was in it.

  

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

**Ashoka, Thirteenth Rock Edict** (c. 250s BCE). The first and most extraordinary record of active Buddhist outreach to the Hellenistic Mediterranean, naming Ptolemy II, Antiochus II, Antigonas Gonatas, Magas of Cyrene, and Alexander II of Epirus as recipients of Buddhist missionaries.

  

**Philo of Alexandria,** ***De Vita Contemplativa*** (1st century CE). The primary description of the Therapeutae — the contemplative community near Lake Mareotis whose practices parallel Buddhist monasticism with remarkable precision.

  

**The** ***Periplus Maris Erythraei*** (1st century CE). The merchant's guide to the Indian Ocean trade, documenting the systematic connection between Egypt and India — hundreds of ships per year, the active circulation of Indo-Greek coinage in Indian ports.

  

**Strabo,** ***Geography*****, Book XV; Cassius Dio, Roman History LIII.** On Zarmanochegas/Zarmarus, his self-immolation in Athens during the reign of Augustus, and his monument in the city.

  

**Plutarch,** ***Life of Alexander*****, Chapter 65.** On the Indian monument in Athens, still known in the first century CE.

### Archaeological Evidence

**Dharma wheel gravestone, Ptolemaic Alexandria** (Ptolemaic period, exact date uncertain). Buddhist gravestone from the Hellenistic capital of Egypt.

  

**Buddha statue, Berenice Troglodytica** (excavated 2022). Found in a temple precinct dedicated to Isis and Serapis, alongside Hindu deities. Confirms Buddhist religious presence in the Egyptian Red Sea trade port.

### Modern Scholarly Works

**McEvilley, Thomas.** ***The Shape of Ancient Thought*****.** Chapters 25–28: the presence of Indian philosophical ideas in the Alexandrian intellectual world; the Therapeutae hypothesis.

  

**Sedlar, Jean W.** ***India and the Greek World: A Study in the Transmission of Culture*****. Rowman & Littlefield, 1980.** Systematic treatment of evidence for Indian-Greek cultural exchange in the Hellenistic period.

  

**Vermes, Geza and Martin Goodman.** ***The Essenes According to the Classical Sources*****. Sheffield Academic Press, 1989.** For context on the Therapeutae within Jewish ascetic traditions.
