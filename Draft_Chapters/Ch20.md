# Chapter 20 — There Were Always "Western" Buddhists

---

> *"The dharma is not the possession of any people or any place.*
> *It belongs to whoever seeks it."*
> — attributed formulation reflecting the Buddha's recorded teaching on the universality of the path

---

## I. The Inventory

Let us take stock of what has been demonstrated.

Scylax of Caryanda — a Greek, in the service of a Persian emperor — sailed the Indus in 519 BCE and opened the first documented channel of systematic contact between the Greek world and the Indian world. The word *Yavana* — Greek — appeared in Sanskrit texts at the turn of the fourth century BCE. Indian troops fought at Plataea, on European soil. The Persepolis tablets recorded Greeks and Indians working in the same workshops under the same administration, drawing the same daily rations.

Then Alexander arrived, and Onesicritus talked to Mandanis the gymnosophist, and Pyrrho of Elis came home having absorbed something that became Western philosophical skepticism — the first identifiable transfer of an Indian philosophical insight into the mainstream of Greek thought.

Then Megasthenes sat in the court of Chandragupta and wrote the first extended Greek account of India from the inside, sending it back to a Hellenistic world that would never quite imagine Asia in the same way again.

Then Diodotus refused the commands of Antioch and founded a Greco-Bactrian kingdom — a state that was simultaneously and authentically Greek and Central Asian, a laboratory in which the first sustained tests of Greek-Indian cultural synthesis were conducted over more than a century of contact and exchange.

Then the Delphic maxims appeared at Ai-Khanoum, carved in stone three thousand miles from Delphi by a man named Clearchos of Soli who had traveled to the ends of the earth because he thought those words were worth carrying.

Then Ashoka's scribes chose *eusebeia* — the Greek word for reverent duty to gods and parents and community — to carry the weight of *dharma* into the Greek-speaking world. Buddhist ethics spoke Greek.

Then Menander sat with Nāgasena and followed the chariot argument to its conclusion — the self as a useful fiction, the chariot as a collection of parts without an essence — and said, at the end of it: *I am yours.* He gave his kingdom over to the Dharma and died, and the cities of his kingdom disputed the honor of enshrining his ashes, exactly as Pali tradition records the cities disputing the ashes of the Buddha, and Plutarch recorded both events in the same breath because the parallel seemed natural to him.

Then thirty thousand Greek monks marched to Sri Lanka, led by Mahadhammarakhita, whose very name — *Maha*, Sanskrit for great; *Dhammarakhita*, protector of the Dharma — told the story of a man who had crossed the entire cognitive distance between the Athens he was descended from and the tradition he had given his life to.

Then Greek hands carved the first anthropomorphic face of the Buddha, modeling it on the conventions of Apollo, giving him the anastole wave in his hair and the Mediterranean proportions of divine beauty, and this face became the face that would travel along the Silk Road to China and Korea and Japan, the face that still gazes down from the altars of temples ten thousand miles from the workshops of Gandhara where it was first imagined.

Then Heracles became Vajrapāṇi and the winds of Greece became the gods of Japan and Corinthian columns held up the sacred space of Buddhist monasteries.

Then Clement of Alexandria wrote the Buddha's name in Greek letters — *Boutta* — in a Christian philosophical text, treating the report of his existence as worthy of a serious theologian's attention.

Then the Buddha became Saint Josaphat, and the Church of Rome placed his feast day in November, and monks across medieval Europe observed it for a thousand years without knowing whose story they were honoring.

This is not a list of footnotes. This is the record of one of the longest, most continuous, most densely documented cultural exchanges in the history of the ancient world. And for most of the last two centuries, it has been treated as an anomaly — a series of exceptions to a rule that the evidence, taken seriously, does not support.

The rule needs to change.

## II. The Manufacture of Difference

The idea that Buddhism is essentially, inherently, naturally Asian — and that Christianity is essentially, inherently, naturally European — is not an ancient idea. It is a modern one. More precisely, it is a colonial one.

Its history can be dated with some precision. In the middle decades of the nineteenth century, as European colonial power was reaching its maximum extent across the continent of Asia, European scholars were simultaneously constructing the intellectual architecture that would explain and justify what European powers were doing on the ground. The two projects were not identical, and the scholars were not simply propagandists. But the categories they built were not innocent, either.

The German-born Oxford scholar Friedrich Max Müller founded what he called the comparative science of religion in the 1860s and 1870s. His *Sacred Books of the East* series — fifty volumes of translated Asian religious texts, begun in 1879 — was a monument of scholarship. It also, almost inadvertently, accomplished something else: it constituted Asian religious traditions as *objects of study*, things that Western scholars examined and translated and categorized, rather than as living traditions that Western people might actually inhabit. Buddhism, in the *Sacred Books of the East*, was what Asians had believed and practiced. It was a subject of philological and historical research.

T.W. Rhys Davids, founding the Pali Text Society in 1881, performed a similar double operation. His translations of Pali Buddhist texts opened them to English-language readers for the first time; his scholarly framework simultaneously marked them as the property of a specific ethnic and cultural tradition. The Pali Text Society was a Western institution studying an Eastern religion. The asymmetry was structural.

Edward Said's *Orientalism*, published in 1978, named the mechanism through which this asymmetry operated. The Orient, Said argued, was not a natural geographical or cultural fact but a constructed category — brought into being by Western scholarship and Western power as the term for everything that Europe was not: timeless where Europe was dynamic, spiritual where Europe was rational, essential where Europe was modern, fixed where Europe was progressive. The Orient was Europe's mirror image, constructed to define Europe by contrast. And Buddhism, by the nineteenth century, had become a prime example of Orientalist categorization: the religion of the timeless, spiritual, essentially non-Western East.

What this construction required was the suppression of the actual historical record. Not a deliberate conspiracy — no one assembled to decide that the Greek Buddhists should be forgotten — but the steady gravitational pull of a framework that made certain facts illegible. The Greek Buddhist monks did not fit the category "Western" as the nineteenth century defined it, because the category had been constructed to exclude exactly that kind of evidence. The Greco-Bactrian kingdoms did not fit the category "Asian" in its proper form, because their coins had Greek on one side. So the coins were displayed in museums as curiosities of Hellenistic diffusion, and the Greek monks were treated as exceptions that proved the rule, and the rule remained standing.

Said was writing about the Islamic world. But the mechanism he identified applies with equal force to the history of Buddhism in the ancient Mediterranean world. The divide between East and West, between Asian religion and Western religion, between what *they* believe and what *we* believe, is not a discovery. It is a construction. And like all constructions, it required labor to build and requires labor to maintain — including the labor of suppressing inconvenient evidence.

## III. What the Coins Know

There is a way of holding two things at once without resolving them into a single thing, and the Indo-Greek coins know how to do it.

Pick up a coin of Menander I — the philosopher-king of the *Milindapañha*, the man who sat with Nāgasena and followed the argument to its conclusion. On one side, the obverse: a Greek royal portrait, Menander in Greek military dress, with a diadem, rendered in the Hellenistic tradition of royal numismatic portraiture. Around the rim, the Greek inscription: *ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΣ ΣΩΤΗΡΟΣ ΜΕΝΑΝΔΡΟΥ* — *of King Menander the Savior*. The epithet *Soter* — Savior — carries the full weight of Hellenistic royal theology; it is the same epithet given to Ptolemy I of Egypt and Antiochus I of Syria, the title of a king whose power is divine protection made manifest.

Turn the coin over. On the reverse: the Dharma wheel, the *dharmachakra*, the symbol of the Buddha's first teaching. Around the rim, the same legend in a different language: Kharoshthi script, the script of the Gandharan Buddhist world, giving the same words in Gāndhārī Prakrit. Two languages, two scripts, two visual traditions, two theological frameworks — on a single object, the size of a thumbnail.

This is not confusion. The people who designed these coins, who struck them and circulated them as instruments of commerce and royal authority across an empire, were not confused about whether they were Greek or Buddhist. They were both. The coin is not a record of identity crisis. It is a record of identity abundance.

Menander's coins are the perfect counter to the colonial construction of religious identity as singular, bounded, essentially attached to a specific ethnic and geographical origin. The coin does not ask: *are you Greek or are you Buddhist?* It holds the Greek king and the Buddhist symbol in the same hand and says: *yes*.

The monk Mahadhammarakhita is the same coin in human form. His name is Sanskrit. His teacher Mahinda, who brought the Dharma to Sri Lanka, was the son of Ashoka. The thirty thousand monks he led to the consecration of the Ruwanwelisaya stupa were called, in the *Mahavamsa*, *Yona* monks — Greek monks. They had taken the precepts of the Vinaya. They had renounced the world. They had made the journey from their homeland to the island at the southern edge of the Indian Ocean to honor a sacred building in a tradition that had no name, in the third century BCE, for the distinction between East and West.

Dharmaraksita — the Greek teacher of *dharma* who appears in the *Mahavamsa* and the *Dipavamsa* as one of the great missionaries sent out by Ashoka's council — preached, according to those texts, in the Aparanta region of western India, in a Greek-speaking community, using philosophical arguments that his students recognized as continuous with the Greek philosophical tradition they had grown up in. He is the first documented instance of a Westerner teaching Buddhist doctrine to other Westerners. He is not an exception. He is the historical proof that the categories of East and West, Western religion and Eastern religion, never applied to the world that actually existed.

## IV. The Persistence of the Truth

The categories were constructed. The evidence was suppressed — not destroyed, because evidence of this kind is very hard to destroy, but marginalized, rendered anomalous, placed in the corner labeled *exceptions* where it would not disturb the larger framework.

But evidence has a way of persisting. The coins persist in museum collections, bilingual and biscriptal, indifferent to the categories that would prefer they be less complicated. The *Mahavamsa* persists, with its matter-of-fact account of thirty thousand Greek monks. The name *Josaphat* persists, carrying *Bodhisattva* in its syllables for anyone who knows how to read it. The face of the Buddha persists in the temples of Japan, still wearing the physiognomy of an Apollo.

What has changed, in this century, is the willingness to read the evidence as what it is.

The Buddhist doctrine of *anattā* — non-self — teaches that there is no fixed, permanent, unitary essence underlying what we call a person. What we experience as a self is a process, not an entity: a stream of changing moments that we retrospectively organize into the fiction of a continuous "I." This teaching, when Menander encountered it in the chariot argument, struck him as both philosophically devastating and existentially liberating. He recognized in it something the Greek tradition had also approached, from different angles, in different vocabulary — Heraclitean flux, Skeptic suspension of judgment, the Socratic awareness that the examined life unravels all the certainties we thought we possessed.

The same logic applies to collective identity. What we call "Greek civilization" is not a fixed essence that traveled unchanged from Athens to Bactria to Gandhara and back. It was a process — a stream of cultural practice that changed shape at every encounter, with Persian administration, Indian philosophy, Buddhist soteriology, the artistic conventions of the Central Asian plateau, the trade-road aesthetics of the Kushan Empire. The Greece that encountered India was not the Greece of Homer. The India that encountered Greece was not the India of the Vedas. Both were already transformed; both were transformed further; both produced something that neither had been before.

This is not a story of loss. It is a story of vitality. Civilizations that enclose themselves, that build walls around their identities to keep the contamination of contact at bay, tend toward rigidity and fragility. Civilizations that remain permeable — that allow encounter to do its work, that are willing to recognize truth in a form that arrives from elsewhere — tend toward depth and range.

The ancient world that produced the Greco-Buddhist synthesis was, in this sense, more sophisticated than the nineteenth-century colonial world that tried to categorize it. The people who struck the coins with Greek on one side and Kharoshthi on the other were not confused about who they were. They knew exactly who they were: people for whom both things were true.

There were always Western Buddhists. There were always Eastern Christians. The Therapeutae practiced in the Egyptian desert before the Desert Fathers. Clement of Alexandria knew the Buddha's name. A medieval monk in a European scriptorium spent his devotional labor copying the life of Siddhartha Gautama. Pyrrho came home from India with something he had not left Greece with. Menander gave his ashes to the cities that loved him.

The categories are the problem. The history, read without them, tells a different story — one in which the encounter between the Greek world and the Indian world is not an anomaly but a constitutive fact of Western civilization, as foundational and as formative as the encounter between Athens and Jerusalem that a later age would make so much of.

Two rivers. One meeting place. The same water, wearing different names.

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

**Said, Edward W. *Orientalism*.** Pantheon, 1978. The foundational analysis of the mechanism by which Western scholarship constructed "the East" as a category of colonial difference. The argument applies to Buddhist studies with the same force Said applied it to Islamic studies.

**Müller, Friedrich Max. *The Sacred Books of the East* (50 vols.).** Oxford University Press, 1879–1910. The monument of nineteenth-century comparative religion that simultaneously opened Asian religious texts to Western readers and constituted them as objects of Eastern otherness.

**Rhys Davids, T.W. *Buddhism: Its History and Literature*. American Lectures on the History of Religions, 1896.** The founding text of English-language academic Buddhist studies; its framing assumptions reward critical reading alongside Said.

**Tarn, W.W. *The Greeks in Bactria and India* (2nd ed.).** Cambridge University Press, 1951. The standard historical treatment of the Indo-Greek kingdoms; dated in some respects but still valuable for the numismatic and documentary evidence.

**Fussman, Gérard. "The Mat Devī and Bilingual Coins: The Iconography of Royal Power in the Kushan Empire."** Various articles on the Indo-Greek and Kushan numismatic tradition; the bilingual coins are discussed in detail throughout the specialized literature.

**On the *Mahavamsa* evidence for Greek monks:** Geiger, Wilhelm, trans. *The Mahavamsa, or the Great Chronicle of Ceylon*. Pali Text Society, 1912. Chapter 29 gives the account of Mahadhammarakhita and the Yona monks at the Ruwanwelisaya consecration.

**Beckwith, Christopher I. *Greek Buddha: Pyrrho's Encounter with Early Buddhism in Central Asia*.** Princeton University Press, 2015. The most sustained modern argument for the direct philosophical influence of early Buddhist thought on Pyrrho of Elis.
