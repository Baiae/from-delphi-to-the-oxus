# Chapter 19 — Barlaam and Josaphat

---

> *"And so it came about that the prince Josaphat, renouncing the kingdom and all the world,*
> *went forth into the desert to seek the wisdom that Barlaam had shown him."*
> — *Barlaam and Ioasaph*, attributed to John of Damascus, c. 8th century CE

---

## I. The Monk Who Did Not Know

Sometime in the eleventh or twelfth century — the exact date is lost, as the dates of so many medieval acts of copying are lost — a monk sat in a scriptorium in western Europe and bent over a sheet of vellum. Around him, the small sounds of the monastery: the creak of a stool, the distant bell for the office, the scratch of other pens. Before him, the Latin text he had been set to copy.

The work was a hagiography — a saint's life — of the kind that formed the core curriculum of medieval religious reading. The saint was a prince named Josaphat, an Indian king's son who had been sequestered from the world by his father and who had nonetheless encountered suffering, been taught wisdom by a hermit named Barlaam, renounced his kingdom, and gone to live in the desert as a contemplative, achieving a kind of sanctity that the Church had recognized by placing his feast day in the calendar. November 27th. The monk copied the letters with the diligence of a man performing a devotional act, which is what he believed he was doing.

He did not know — could not have known, in the eleventh century, before the comparative scholarship of the Renaissance and its successors made such knowledge thinkable — that the story he was copying with such careful piety was the story of Siddhartha Gautama.

He did not know that the prince's miraculous birth and the prophecy of his future greatness echoed the accounts of the Buddha's nativity in the *Buddhacarita*, written in Sanskrit by the poet Aśvaghoṣa somewhere in the first or second century of the common era. He did not know that the father who built a palace to sequester his son from all sight of suffering was a translation of King Śuddhodana, the father who surrounded the young Siddhartha with pleasure and ease and commanded that no sight of age or illness or death should reach the prince. He did not know that the three encounters with suffering — the old man, the sick man, the corpse — through which Josaphat's awakening was triggered were the three of the four sights through which Siddhartha's awakening began, carried intact across a thousand years and three languages.

He did not know that the hermit Barlaam, who disguised himself as a merchant to gain access to the sequestered prince and taught him the wisdom of renunciation, was performing precisely the function of Kondañña and the five ascetics: the teacher who meets the seeker at the threshold.

He was copying the life of the Buddha. He thought he was honoring a Christian saint.

Both of these things were true.

---

## II. The Story Itself

To understand what happened, you must first feel the weight of the parallels — not as a scholar cataloguing correspondences, but as a reader encountering two texts and watching the same shape emerge from beneath different surfaces.

In the *Buddhacarita*, the birth of Siddhartha is attended by portents. A white elephant enters his mother's side in a dream. Wise men prophesy his future greatness: he will be either a great king or a great sage. His father, hearing this, chooses the first option and sets about constructing a world from which the second possibility is excluded. He builds palaces of pleasure. He surrounds his son with youth and beauty and abundance. He commands that no sight of suffering shall reach the prince. Old age, sickness, and death are to be kept outside the walls.

In *Barlaam and Ioasaph*, the birth of the prince Josaphat is attended by portents. Astrologers at his father's court prophesy that the child will be a great man — not, however, in the way his father King Abenner of India imagines. He will renounce the world. He will become a Christian holy man. His father, hearing this with dread, builds a palace of sequestration and keeps his son within it, surrounded by young companions chosen for their health and vigor, forbidden to speak of age or illness or the Christian faith.

The parallels are not approximate. They are structural.

When the young Siddhartha eventually escapes his father's careful construction — the first chariot journey, the first sight of the world as it actually is — he encounters, in succession: a bent and white-haired old man, moving with painful difficulty; a man wasting with illness; a corpse, attended by mourners. Three of the four sights. The fourth is the wandering ascetic, the man who has renounced the world — and it is this sight that suggests to Siddhartha that renunciation is possible, that there is a path through the suffering he has just witnessed.

When Josaphat, through a sequence of circumstances his father cannot prevent, is eventually brought face to face with the world outside his palace, his encounters follow the same grammar: a blind man and a leper, a man bowed with age. Three encounters with the face of suffering. And then, the teacher who appears: not a wandering ascetic glimpsed on the road, but the hermit Barlaam, who has traveled through the desert to find the sequestered prince because he knows, somehow, that the prince is ready.

The teacher function in both texts is the same. Barlaam teaches through parables — extended allegorical stories, the parable being the preferred pedagogical instrument of wisdom traditions across the ancient world, from the Upanishads to the Sermon on the Mount. The *Barlaam and Ioasaph* text contains dozens of parables, several of which — the story of the man fleeing a unicorn who falls into a pit and, while clinging to a root that is being gnawed by mice, pauses to lick honey from the rock face — have been traced to earlier Buddhist sources. The parable of the unicorn appears in the Buddhist *Mahābhārata* and in a Jain parallel; it traveled the same road as the rest of the story.

The ending, too, rhymes. Josaphat renounces his kingdom — as Siddhartha renounced his — and goes into the desert to live as a contemplative. The story closes with his death in a posture of devotion and his subsequent miracles. The shape of the life is the same shape: a young man who has everything the world can give, who encounters the truth that the world's gifts cannot finally satisfy, who finds a teacher, who renounces, who achieves. The particulars are different. The shape is identical.

That shape is not a copy. It is a recognition.

---

## III. The Road It Traveled

The transmission of this story from the forests of northern India to the scriptoria of medieval Europe is one of the most intricate acts of cultural relay that the ancient and medieval world produced. Each stage of the journey can be partially traced, though not without gaps.

The story begins with Aśvaghoṣa's *Buddhacarita* — the Acts of the Buddha — composed in Sanskrit in the first or second century CE, the first great literary biography of Siddhartha Gautama. It was a work of considerable artistry, modeled partly on the conventions of the Sanskrit *mahākāvya* epic form, and it circulated widely in the Buddhist world. The *Lalitavistara* and other Pali and Sanskrit biographical traditions amplified and elaborated the story. By the fifth and sixth centuries, the life of the Buddha was as well-known in Buddhist literary culture as the life of Alexander was in the Hellenistic world.

From India, the story entered the Iranian world. Somewhere in the Sassanid Persian court — which maintained connections with Buddhist scholarship through the Central Asian trade networks — a version of the Buddha's biography was translated into Middle Persian, the language known as Pahlavi. The date of this translation is uncertain: most scholars place it in the sixth or seventh century CE. At the Sassanid court, Buddhist texts were not unknown; the Silk Road ran through Persian territory, Buddhist monasteries operated in the empire's eastern provinces, and the Sassanid kings were, as a matter of policy, collectors of knowledge from across the known world. The *Buddhacarita* would have been, to a Persian court intellectual, an interesting text from the eastern frontier of learning.

From the Pahlavi version, the story passed into the Arabic-speaking world. The Arabic text, known as *Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Yūdāsaf* — the Book of Bilawhar and Yudasaf — circulated widely in the Islamic world from approximately the eighth century CE. The precise mechanism of this passage, from Pahlavi to Arabic, is the most debated link in the chain, and the most important.

---

## IV. The Missing Link

The hypothesis that best accounts for the Pahlavi-to-Arabic transmission is one that, like all the most illuminating hypotheses in this story, requires the scholar to look not where the light is brightest but where the traffic was heaviest.

The Manichaean missionaries.

Manichaeism was founded by the prophet Mani in Mesopotamia in the third century CE. Mani was himself a figure of extraordinary range: raised in a Jewish-Christian baptismal community in Babylon, he traveled to northwestern India in his youth and absorbed Buddhist teachings there before returning to Mesopotamia to found what he explicitly described as the final, universal religion — one that incorporated and superseded all the partial truths of the traditions that had preceded it. In the Manichaean scripture, the Buddha appears alongside Zoroaster and Jesus as one of Mani's predecessors: a prophet of the light who had taught partial truth to his own people, now superseded by Mani's more complete revelation.

This made the Manichaeans uniquely positioned as transmitters of religious narrative. They were, by theological conviction, synthesists. They read Buddhist texts because their religion told them that the Buddhist tradition contained truth worth preserving. Their missionaries were among the most mobile people in the ancient world — traveling deliberately along the Silk Road routes, from the Mediterranean to China, carrying their scriptures and their narratives and their commercial relationships with them. They had communities in Persia, in the Arabian peninsula, in Central Asia, in China. They were, in the most literal sense, a religion of transmission.

The hypothesis that Manichaean intermediaries carried the Buddhist narrative tradition from the Pahlavi world into the Arabic literary world cannot be proven from the surviving documentary record. The transmission left no notarized receipts. But the structural fit is close to perfect: a syncretic religion with Buddhist content in its scriptural core, a missionary network that ran exactly along the routes the story needed to travel, and a cultural moment — the seventh and eighth centuries CE — in which Manichaean communities were active in precisely the zones where the transmission appears to have occurred.

The story arrived in Arabic wearing different clothes, as stories do at every border. The *Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Yūdāsaf* is not a translation of a Buddhist text. It is a new text, shaped by its new religious and cultural context, retaining the structural bones of the *Buddhacarita* story while dressing those bones in the flesh of Islamic literary convention. The teacher Bilawhar and the prince Yudasaf move through a world that is recognizably the world of Arabic wisdom literature. But the four sights are still there. The sequestering father is still there. The renunciation is still there. The shape persists beneath the new clothing.

From Arabic, the story traveled to Georgia. The Georgian text, known as the *Balavariani*, was produced in the tenth century CE, translated from the Arabic by the Georgian monk Euthymios on Mount Athos. The Georgian tradition treated the story as a tale of Christian conversion — which is, by this point in its journey, what it had largely become. From Georgia, the Greek text emerged: *Barlaam kai Ioasaph*, the version that would for centuries be attributed to John of Damascus, one of the great Church Fathers of the Eastern tradition, lending the story an authority it might otherwise not have acquired.

From Greek, translation into Latin. From Latin, the explosion across medieval European literary culture that made *Barlaam and Josaphat* one of the most widely read texts of the Middle Ages — translated into French, Italian, English, German, Provençal, Hebrew, and Icelandic. Versions appeared in the *Golden Legend*, the great medieval compendium of saints' lives that was, for centuries, among the most copied books in Europe.

The story had traveled from a forest in northern India to a scriptorium in Iceland. It had worn six languages and traveled for a millennium and a half. It had been Buddhist, Manichaean, Arabic, Georgian, Greek, and Latin. At each stage, it had been transformed. At each stage, the shape had persisted.

---

## V. The Saint in the Calendar

At the end of this journey, the Buddha stood in the calendar of the Roman Catholic Church.

His feast day was November 27th. Both the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches celebrated Saints Barlaam and Ioasaph — Barlaam and Josaphat in the Latin West. The feast was observed in monasteries across Europe. Relics attributed to Josaphat were venerated. Churches were dedicated in his name. The man who had sat under a tree in what is now Bihar and arrived at the Four Noble Truths was, for several centuries, honored in the same liturgical breath as Peter and Paul.

This was not deception. The monks who observed his feast day were not secretly Buddhists, nor had they been tricked into venerating a foreign deity. They encountered the story of Josaphat as a story of Christian conversion and Christian sanctity, and they were not wrong to do so: the story, by the time it reached them, was genuinely a Christian story. The transformation was real. What it demonstrates is not the credulity of medieval Christians but the depth of a narrative truth that traveled so well precisely because it was true in a register that transcended any single religious framing.

Then scholarship caught up with the story.

In the seventeenth century, the Portuguese historian Diogo de Couto, working in Goa and familiar with Buddhist traditions from his years in the East, noted the parallels between Josaphat's life and the Buddha's. In the eighteenth century, the Portuguese scholar Francisco da Horta Osório made the connection more explicit. In the nineteenth century, as comparative philology came into its own as a discipline, scholars began tracing the transmission chain with something approaching the rigor the evidence warranted. The Sanskrit scholar Max Müller contributed. The Buddhist scholar Eugène Burnouf worked on the problem. By the 1880s and 1890s, the main lines of the transmission were established beyond reasonable doubt.

The Church, presented with this evidence, did what institutions generally do when confronted with inconvenient history: it noted the finding, did not contest it, and quietly moved on. In the Roman Catholic calendar revision of 1969, Saints Barlaam and Josaphat were removed from the universal calendar. Their commemoration was not formally abolished; it was transferred to local option, which in practice meant that their feast day ceased to be observed in most of the Western Church.

The Buddha was quietly de-canonized. A millennium of unknowing was acknowledged, gently closed, and filed.

---

## VI. The Name

There is a way of reading what we have traced as a curiosity — an interesting accident, an irony of transmission. The story traveled far, arrived unrecognized, was honored for centuries in a case of mistaken identity, and was eventually corrected.

But the name corrects that reading.

*Josaphat.* The Latin form of the name carried into medieval Europe.

Before the Latin: *Ioasaph.* The Greek transliteration, in the text attributed to John of Damascus.

Before the Greek: *Yudasaf* — or *Yudasaf / Budasaf* — the Arabic form, in the *Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Yūdāsaf*. The initial consonant had shifted, in the passage from Pahlavi to Arabic, through a confusion of letters — the Arabic *bā'* and *yā'* are visually similar in certain scripts, and the transmission was oral as well as written. A mistake of copying became a permanent feature of the name.

Before the Arabic: the Pahlavi intermediary form, now mostly lost, carrying the name across from the Sanskrit through the Persian.

Before all of this: the Sanskrit. *Bodhisattva.* The being of enlightenment. The one who has taken the vow to achieve awakening for the sake of all sentient beings. The foundational figure of Mahāyāna Buddhist aspiration.

*Josaphat* is *Bodhisattva*, worn down by a thousand years of translation, reduced to its consonantal skeleton, stripped of its vowels and its meaning and its linguistic home, and still recognizable to anyone who knows where to look.

The name did not travel accidentally. The name traveled because it was the name. Because the story demanded its protagonist be named, and the name that was given him at the story's origin — *Bodhisattva*, the enlightened being — persisted through every transformation. Bilawhar's prince was called Yudasaf because the source he was drawn from called his prince Bodhisattva, and no one at any stage of the transmission had reason to replace a name with a different name. So the name persisted, garbled at each border crossing, unrecognized at each new destination, but present. Encoded in the letters.

*Josaphat.* Say it slowly. The journey is in the syllables.

The monk in the scriptorium did not know. He was copying the life of the Buddha. He was honoring a Christian saint. He was, though he could not have known this, honoring the same truth that a man under a tree in Bihar had arrived at fifteen hundred years before him: that suffering is real, that the cause of suffering can be understood, that a way through suffering exists, and that the way through is a form of release that no palace and no sequestration can permanently prevent.

The monk copied the letters. The truth traveled. This is the most compressed proof of everything this book has argued.

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

**Aśvaghoṣa, *Buddhacarita*** (*Acts of the Buddha*, c. 1st–2nd century CE). The Sanskrit source from which the transmission begins. Best English translation: E.H. Johnston, *The Buddhacarita, or Acts of the Buddha* (Punjab University Press, 1936; repr. Motilal Banarsidass).

***Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Yūdāsaf*** (Arabic, c. 8th century CE). The Arabic version of the story, the linchpin of the transmission. Critical Arabic text with analysis in Daniel Gimaret's study.

***Barlaam kai Ioasaph*** (Greek, c. 7th–8th century; traditionally attributed to John of Damascus; now generally attributed to Euthymios of Iberia or his circle). Standard English translation: G.R. Woodward and H. Mattingly, *St. John Damascene: Barlaam and Ioasaph* (Loeb Classical Library, 1914).

**Lang, David Marshall. *The Balavariani (Barlaam and Ioasaph): A Tale from the Christian East*.** University of California Press, 1966. The definitive scholarly treatment of the transmission history and the Georgian link.

**Gimaret, Daniel. *Le livre de Bilawhar et Bûdâsf: selon la version arabe ismaélienne*.** Droz, 1971. The critical study of the Arabic text.

**Wilkinson, Tracy. "How the Buddha Became a Christian Saint." Various popular accounts in the scholarly press summarize the transmission history; the most rigorous version is still Lang's 1966 study.**

**Couto, Diogo de. *Décadas da Ásia*** (1602–1616). The earliest Western scholarly identification of the Josaphat-Buddha parallel, by a Portuguese historian writing in Goa.

**On Manichaeism as a transmission vehicle:** Klimkeit, Hans-Joachim. *Gnosis on the Silk Road: Gnostic Parables, Hymns and Prayers from Central Asia*. HarperCollins, 1993. For the Manichaean synthesis and missionary networks, Richard Foltz, *Religions of the Silk Road* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2000) provides the clearest overview.
