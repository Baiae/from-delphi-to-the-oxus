# Chapter 17 — Monasticism Without Borders

---

> *"A monk must have no possessions, must beg for food, must sleep on the ground, must be indifferent to cold and heat, to pain and pleasure."*
> — Mandanis, speaking to Onesicritus, 327 BCE (Strabo, *Geography*)

> *"Give up all for the sake of God: possessions, relatives, body. For the one who has renounced everything has received everything."*
> — Abba Moses, Desert Father, c. 300 CE

## I. Two Traditions, One Practice

The Desert Fathers appeared in Egypt and Syria in the late third and early fourth centuries CE. Driven by what they described as an encounter with the living God, they left the cities and the social world — left family, property, ambition, the whole apparatus of ordinary Roman life — and went into the desert to live alone or in small communities, in radical poverty, sustained by a few dates and a little water, spending their days in prayer and manual labor and the practice of what they called *hesychia* — stillness, interior silence, the quieting of the mind that they believed was the prerequisite for encountering the divine.

They were, in their practices, almost indistinguishable from the Indian gymnosophists who had astonished the Greeks six hundred years earlier.

The parallels between early Christian monasticism and Indian asceticism are so numerous and so precise that they have attracted scholarly attention for two centuries, and the debate about their significance — coincidence? parallel development? direct influence? — has never been entirely settled. But the parallels are real, and they deserve careful attention.

One preliminary observation is worth making before the catalogue begins. The Desert Fathers did not emerge in Jerusalem, or in Rome, or in Antioch, or in any of the other great centers of early Christian life. They emerged in Egypt — in the country that was the western terminus of the Indian Ocean trade, the country where Buddhist gravestones had been found in Ptolemaic Alexandria, where an active contemplative community called the Therapeutae had been practicing a form of monastic life for centuries before any of the Desert Fathers were born. The geography is not coincidental. The Desert Fathers lived in the downstream culture of the world that this book has been tracing.

## II. The Therapeutae: Before the Desert

Before the Desert Fathers, there were the Therapeutae.

Philo of Alexandria — the Jewish philosopher who was a contemporary of Jesus, writing in the first century CE — described the Therapeutae in a treatise he called *De Vita Contemplativa*, On the Contemplative Life. It is one of the most extraordinary documents of the ancient world, and one of the most puzzling. Philo claimed that the Therapeutae were Jewish. But what he described was something that had no real precedent in the Jewish tradition.

The Therapeutae lived near Lake Mareotis, a few miles southwest of Alexandria. They had renounced personal property before joining the community; they had renounced family obligations. They lived in individual dwellings that Philo called *monastēria* — individual cells, separate but arranged in proximity. During the six days of the week, they remained in their cells, in solitary study and contemplation. On the seventh day they assembled for communal prayer and philosophical discussion. At longer intervals, they gathered for a communal feast.

The communal feast was conducted according to an elaborate protocol. Senior members reclined; junior members served standing. The presiding elder discoursed on scripture, using the method of allegorical interpretation — finding the spiritual meaning behind the literal text, a method that required years of training to master. No wine was consumed until the philosophical discourse was complete. After the discourse, they ate with sobriety and ate little. After the meal, they sang together in alternating choirs, men and women both, in a practice that Philo describes as a response to the original crossing of the Red Sea but that in its structure resembles the antiphonal chanting of Buddhist and later Christian monastic communities.

Both men and women were members of the community. The women's cells were separated from the men's by a partition, but they shared the communal practice — the same study, the same fasting, the same feast. This integration of women as full contemplatives, equal in their practice if separated in their cells, had no precedent in Jewish religious organization. It is the norm in the Buddhist sangha, where nuns have been ordained since the time of the Buddha himself.

The individual cell for solitary practice; the communal assembly for shared ritual; the hierarchy of senior and junior practitioners; the emphasis on study and contemplation as modes of spiritual development; the physical austerity; the renunciation of personal property; the integration of women: each of these features has a parallel in Buddhist monastic organization, and some of them — particularly the combination of solitary hermitic practice with structured communal assembly — are not characteristic of any other Mediterranean religious organization of the period.

Philo offered two etymologies for the name *Therapeutae*: "healers" or "servants of God," both derivable from the Greek verb *therapeuein*. Both etymologies are linguistically plausible. But the community's practices point toward a different possibility — that the name was a Hellenized rendering of *Theravada*, the name of the oldest school of Buddhist monasticism, the school whose institutional traditions most closely match what Philo describes. The Theravada — "the teaching of the elders" — had been the dominant school in the Hellenistic northwest of the Indian world, the school most exposed to Greek cultural influence, the school whose monks were most likely to have traveled westward along the trade routes.

This etymology cannot be proven and remains contested in the scholarly literature. But it cannot be dismissed. And the alternative — that a community practicing Buddhist-style monasticism appeared spontaneously in Alexandria in the first century CE, in a city saturated with Indian influence, with no connection to the tradition that had been practicing this form of life for five hundred years — requires a credulity about coincidence that the evidence does not justify.

## III. The Parallels

The Desert Fathers who emerged from Egypt in the late third century were the heirs of this longer tradition — whether or not they knew it. And the parallels between their practice and the practice of the Buddhist sangha are worth cataloguing precisely, not as a list of superficial similarities but as a pattern that requires explanation.

Both traditions practice *poverty*: the complete renunciation of personal property, not as an expression of economic necessity but as a deliberate spiritual discipline. The Desert Father who kept a possession beyond what was strictly necessary for survival was understood to have compromised the very capacity for attention that the practice was meant to develop. The Buddhist monk who accumulated private property violated the Vinaya. The logic is identical: the attachment to things is itself the obstacle.

Both traditions practice *begging*: the monk does not work for food but receives what is offered, making himself dependent on the goodwill of laypeople in a relationship understood as spiritually beneficial to both parties. The giver gains merit; the monk maintains the humility of reception. The Vinaya specifies this in great detail; the Desert Father sayings record it with the same precision.

Both traditions emphasize *solitude and silence*: the interior life cannot develop amid the noise and distraction of ordinary social existence; withdrawal from society is not the goal of the practice but its prerequisite. Abba Arsenius, one of the most famous of the Desert Fathers, reportedly heard a voice that told him: "Arsenius, flee, be silent, be at peace — these are the roots of sinlessness." The Buddhist tradition uses different language but makes the same claim: the first task of the meditator is to create conditions in which attention can stabilize.

Both traditions practice *fasting and physical austerity*: the body's demands are to be moderated, not indulged; the cultivation of indifference to comfort and discomfort develops the capacity to remain present that is the practical prerequisite of both traditions.

Both traditions cultivate *equanimity* — what the Greek tradition called *apatheia*, the absence of passion-driven disturbance, and what the Pali tradition called *upekkhā*, the balanced, undisturbed awareness that sees clearly because it is not pushed and pulled by preferences and aversions. The Desert Father sought *apatheia* not as emotional flatness but as the interior stillness that made genuine love possible. The Buddhist meditator cultivated *upekkhā* as one of the four divine abodes — the qualities of heart that accompany awakening. The words are different. The quality of attention they describe is the same.

And both traditions produce, in their most advanced practitioners, a quality of presence that contemporaries describe in strikingly similar terms: a quality of settled, lucid, untroubled awareness that others find both magnetic and somewhat unsettling, because it suggests a freedom from the ordinary anxieties of human existence that most people cannot imagine.

## IV. The Transmission Question

The scholarly debate about the relationship between Indian asceticism and Christian monasticism has taken several forms.

The strongest claim argues for direct historical transmission: that specific practices and possibly specific texts traveled from India through Persia and Egypt and arrived in the Christian monastic tradition through identifiable intermediaries. Proponents point to the Therapeutae, to the presence of Indian traders and philosophers in Alexandria, to the gymnosophist accounts that were widely read in the educated Hellenistic world, to the fact that the Desert Fathers emerged in Egypt, of all the places in the Roman world, rather than in Rome or Antioch or the heartland of Christian institutional life.

Against this position, scholars have argued for parallel development: that extreme asceticism and contemplative practice are universal features of religious life, emerging wherever a tradition takes the interior life seriously, and that the parallels between Indian and Christian monasticism reflect nothing more than the convergence of any two traditions that pursue the same fundamental human aspiration through disciplined practice.

There is a more precise way to state what the Desert Fathers and the Buddhist contemplatives actually shared — one that goes beyond the inventory of similar practices to the underlying conviction that animated them. And this more precise statement brings us to the philosophical heart of the matter.

Both traditions understood that the deepest reality cannot be known by thinking about it. The Greek philosophical vocabulary had a word for this: *gnosis*. Not knowledge in the ordinary sense — not the accumulation of facts or the mastery of arguments. *Gnosis* was the knowledge that comes from conforming to reality rather than merely describing it; the understanding that changes not just what you know but what you are. Plotinus, writing in Alexandria in the third century CE — Plotinus, who had tried to accompany a Roman military expedition to Persia specifically to reach India and study Indian philosophy, and who had barely escaped with his life when the expedition failed — took this to its logical conclusion: you cannot think your way to the ultimate reality, because thought always divides and distinguishes, and ultimate reality is undivided. You can only *be* one with it.

The Desert Fathers, emerging in Egypt in the generation after Plotinus, were pursuing the same thing under a different name. Their word was *theosis* — deification, the transformation of the human person through participation in the divine. Not metaphor, not aspiration: an actual change in the constitution of the self, achieved through decades of practice in solitude, silence, fasting, and prayer. The goal was not to know God as an object of theological inquiry. The goal was to be so thoroughly conformed to the divine nature that the distinction between the knower and the known dissolved.

The Buddhist tradition had been pursuing the same dissolution for five hundred years before the Desert Fathers were born, and it had its own word for what was achieved: *prajna* — the non-dual wisdom that arises when the practitioner has so thoroughly dismantled the ego's claim to be a fixed, separate entity that the usual boundary between self and world no longer operates as it did. The deepest knowing requires the dissolution of the knower. Both traditions arrived at this paradox. Both traditions built their institutional life — the monastery, the cell, the Rule, the Vinaya — around the practical question of how to create the conditions in which such dissolution becomes possible.

Plotinus tried to reach India. He did not succeed. But the ideas that he was reaching toward — ideas that had been fully articulated in the Indian philosophical tradition for centuries — were already present in the Alexandrian atmosphere through the channels this book has been tracing: through Bardesanes's account of the Indian monks he had met, through the Therapeutae, through the Buddhist gravestones and the Buddha statue at Berenice, through the long-established presence of Indian traders and teachers in the city. The Desert Fathers emerged in Egypt, in the same cultural world where Clement had written the Buddha's name, where the philosophical tradition of Plotinus was in the air. The specific practices overlap in ways that are difficult to explain by independent development alone.

But more important than the question of transmission is the recognition of what was being transmitted: not techniques, but a shared understanding that human beings are capable of a kind of knowing that transforms them — and that this capacity, wherever it appears, follows recognizable laws, produces recognizable fruits, and points toward the same territory.

*Gnosis*. *Theosis*. *Prajna*. Three words for the same shore, reached by three different routes, in three different vessels, by three traditions that did not need to share a common origin to have been, in the deepest sense, making the same journey.

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

**Philo of Alexandria, *De Vita Contemplativa*** (1st century CE). The primary description of the Therapeutae community near Lake Mareotis — the most detailed account of a pre-Christian contemplative community surviving from the ancient world.

**Sayings of the Desert Fathers (*Apophthegmata Patrum*)** (c. 4th–5th century CE). The primary collection of Desert Father sayings and practices; the sayings of Abba Arsenius on solitude, silence, and peace are among the most famous.

**Athanasius of Alexandria, *Life of Antony*** (c. 360 CE). The foundational text of Christian monastic hagiography, establishing the model for the solitary contemplative life.

**Strabo, *Geography*, Book XV** (quoting Onesicritus on Mandanis). The primary Greek account of gymnosophist practice for comparison — the first extended description of Indian asceticism available to the Greek-speaking world.

**Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus*** (c. 3rd century CE). Records Plotinus's unsuccessful attempt to accompany the Roman expedition against Persia in order to reach India and study Indian philosophy directly.

### Modern Scholarly Works

**Dechow, Jon F. *Dogma and Mysticism in Early Christianity*. Mercer University Press, 1988.** Context for the ascetic practices of early Christianity.

**Griggs, C. Wilfred. *Early Egyptian Christianity*. Brill, 1990.** The Egyptian background of Christian monasticism.

**McEvilley, Thomas. *The Shape of Ancient Thought*.** Chapters 31–33: Indian asceticism and Christian monasticism — the fullest scholarly treatment of the parallels and the transmission question.

**Vermes, Geza and Martin Goodman. *The Essenes According to the Classical Sources*. Sheffield Academic Press, 1989.** Context for the Therapeutae within the broader tradition of Jewish ascetic communities.
