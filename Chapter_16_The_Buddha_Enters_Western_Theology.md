# Chapter 16 — The Buddha Enters Western Theology

  

*"Some, too, of the Indians obey the precepts of Buddha, whom, on account of his extraordinary sanctity, they have raised to divine honours."* — Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, c. 200 CE

  

## I. Tableau: Alexandria, c. 200 CE

In a study in Alexandria, in the early years of the third century CE, an influential Christian theologian named Titus Flavius Clemens — known to history as Clement of Alexandria — was writing a work of extraordinary ambition.

  

He called it *Stromata* — "Miscellanies" or "Patchwork" — a word that acknowledged the text's deliberate heterogeneity and defied the convention that philosophy should present itself as a unified system. It was a philosophical defense of Christianity, conducted by someone who had read everything: Greek philosophy, mystery religion, Gnostic speculation, Jewish scripture, Persian Zoroastrianism, Egyptian religion, Indian philosophy. What unified the project was a conviction: that all genuine wisdom, from whatever source, pointed ultimately toward the same truth. Philosophy, Clement argued, was a preparatory gift to the Greeks, as the Law was to the Jews — both of them leading, through different paths, toward the fullness of Christian revelation.

  

To make this argument convincingly, Clement had to take non-Christian wisdom seriously. Not politely, not as a gesture of tolerance, but genuinely — as material with intellectual weight, as evidence of the logos at work in traditions that had not yet received the full Christian revelation. He read the Stoics, the Platonists, the Pythagoreans. He engaged with the Gnostic traditions that were competing with orthodox Christianity for the allegiance of educated Mediterranean people. He drew on Jewish scripture and Jewish philosophy — on Philo, whose allegorical method he inherited and developed. He gathered accounts of Persian religion and Egyptian wisdom. And he gathered what he could about the philosophical traditions of India.

  

This was not a casual intellectual exercise. Clement was writing at a moment when Christianity was fighting for its intellectual credibility in the most sophisticated city in the world. The *Stromata* was an argument that Christianity was not the religion of the uneducated and the superstitious — that it was capable of engaging with Plato and Aristotle and Pythagoras on their own terms and demonstrating that what they had been reaching toward, the logos they had dimly perceived, Christianity had fully revealed. To make that argument, Clement needed to show that the logos had been working everywhere — in every tradition, in every culture, in every genuine instance of human wisdom. The Indian world was part of that everywhere.

  

He was writing in Alexandria — the library was still accessible, and its contents were available through the scholars who had spent generations working with them. He was writing in a city where Indian merchants and teachers had been arriving for centuries, where an ascetic community of disputed origin was practicing contemplative life outside the city walls, where Buddhist gravestones had been left by people who had settled and died there. He was writing in the intellectual atmosphere that this book has been tracing: the atmosphere in which Ashoka's dharma had, in some diluted and transformed form, been percolating westward since the third century BCE.

  

And in the course of this project, working through his catalogue of non-Christian wisdom, he reached for an example from the Indian world. He mentioned the Buddha.

  

## II. The First Mention

Clement's reference to the Buddha is brief. It is embedded in a discussion of "barbarian philosophers" — his term for the non-Greek, non-Roman philosophical traditions that he found worth engaging — and it comes in the same passage in which he discusses the Persian Magi, the Druids, the Gymnosophists, and the Jewish tradition.

  

He identifies two groups of Indian philosophers: the *Brahmins* and the *Sarmanae* — his transliteration of the Sanskrit *śramaṇa*, the wandering ascetics, the monks. And he adds a specific note about one teacher:

  

*"Some, too, of the Indians obey the precepts of Buddha (Βούττα), whom, on account of his extraordinary sanctity, they have raised to divine honours."*

  

*Boutta*. It is the first time the Buddha's name appears in Christian literature. The Greek transliteration is recognizable despite the distance and the passage of centuries: the consonants intact, the vowel shifted, the name of the Indian teacher landing in a Christian theological text in the city of Egypt at the beginning of the third century CE.

  

Clement did not know much about Buddhism. His account of Indian philosophy is impressionistic and partial, drawn from secondhand sources. He knows the name. He knows the reputation for sanctity. He knows that the Buddha's followers accord him a kind of divine veneration — that they relate to him the way a religious community relates to a god, not merely to an admired teacher.

  

And he finds this intelligible. He does not regard it as bizarre, does not reach for the language of idolatry or superstition. He treats it as one more instance of the human recognition that some lives, by virtue of extraordinary spiritual attainment, partake of something that transcends the merely human. His own tradition had made precisely this claim about Jesus of Nazareth. The form is different; the recognizable shape is the same.

  

The distinction between the *Brahmins* and the *Sarmanae* is itself significant. Clement knows there are different Indian traditions — that the Brahmin philosophical lineage and the śramaṇa renunciant lineage are not the same thing. This level of differentiation suggests a source that had real, if limited, knowledge of the Indian world. He was not working from a single garbled report.

  

## III. The Chain of Transmission

Clement was not working from nothing, and the passage that mentions the Buddha did not arrive in his *Stromata* without preparation. The knowledge of Indian philosophy that circulated in the Mediterranean world in the second and third centuries CE had specific channels; one of the most important was a man named Bardesanes of Edessa.

  

Bardesanes was born in Edessa in 154 CE — in the city of Osroene, in what is now southeastern Turkey, where the Greek-speaking and Aramaic-speaking worlds met. He was a scholar and theologian who worked in Syriac and Aramaic, the languages that connected the Hellenistic Mediterranean to the Persian-speaking world to its east and, through Persia, to India. He had connections to the Parthian court, and through those connections, he had encountered something that the Greek-speaking philosophical world of Alexandria could only read about at second hand.

  

He had met Indians. Specifically: he had met a delegation of Indian philosophers sent to the court of Caesar — one of the embassies that the *Periplus* records as a regular feature of the Indo-Roman relationship. From this encounter — direct, personal contact with Indian teachers — Bardesanes wrote an account of Indian philosophical and social practice that the later philosopher Porphyry quoted extensively in his treatise *On Abstinence from Animal Food*. Through Porphyry's quotation, the substance of Bardesanes's account reached the Greek-reading world.

  

Bardesanes's account distinguished, as Clement also did, between the *Brahmins* and the *Samanaeans* — the śramaṇas, the wandering monks. Of the Samanaeans, Bardesanes described the following: they had no personal possessions; they received food as a gift from those they taught, not as payment; they did not eat meat; they lived in communities outside the cities; they spent their time in contemplation and teaching; they treated their senior members with a deference that was both social and philosophical — the elder was the teacher, and the teacher was the person whose life most fully expressed the understanding that the tradition was trying to transmit.

  

This is a description of Buddhist monasticism. Not approximate, not evocative — precise. The Vinaya, the Buddhist code of monastic discipline, specifies exactly these arrangements: the prohibition on private property, the dependence on lay generosity for food, the vegetarian diet (in some traditions), the communal structure outside the city. Bardesanes had met Buddhist monks, spoken with them through interpreters, and transmitted a recognizable account of their practice to the Syriac-speaking world.

  

From Edessa, this account traveled. It reached Porphyry, who was working in the third century CE. It shaped the intellectual atmosphere in which Clement was writing at the turn of that century. The Buddha's name appears in the *Stromata* not as a pure invention, not as a garbled rumor, but as the visible peak of a wave of information about the Indian world that had been building since Ashoka's missionaries set out for the Mediterranean and Alexander's soldiers came home from the banks of the Indus.

  

## IV. What This Moment Means

Clement's mention of the Buddha is not, in itself, a major philosophical event. It is a data point — one piece of evidence in a larger argument.

  

But as a data point, it is extremely significant. It tells us that by 200 CE, the Buddha's name and reputation had reached the intellectual capital of the western Christian world, carried through the specific chain of transmission that runs from Ashoka's missions to Bardesanes's encounter with the Indian delegation to Porphyry's quotation to the Alexandrian theological atmosphere in which Clement was working. It tells us that a sophisticated Christian theologian found this information worth including in a serious philosophical work — not as an aside, not as a curiosity, but as one more instance of the human recognition of extraordinary sanctity.

  

And it tells us something about Clement himself: about the kind of intellectual project he was engaged in, and why it required the Buddha's name. Clement was arguing that the logos — the divine rational principle that governed the universe — had been accessible to all human beings throughout history, preparing the ground for the full revelation that Christianity embodied. Every genuine insight, from every tradition, was a gift of the same logos. The Buddha's extraordinary sanctity, recognized by his followers as something more than human — this was, in Clement's framework, the logos at work in the Indian world, preparing Indian hearts for what Christianity was now offering to everyone.

  

This is a generous reading of the Buddha, whatever its theological limitations. And it is the first one in the Western Christian tradition. But it is worth pausing on what it required Clement to recognize — and what it prevented him from recognizing.

  

What it required: that a teacher who emerged from a non-Greek, non-Jewish, non-Persian tradition could nonetheless have achieved something genuinely valuable. That the logos was not the exclusive property of any one cultural tradition. That wisdom and sanctity were portable — that they showed up where the conditions for them existed, regardless of whether the tradition that produced them was connected to the traditions Clement considered primary. The Buddha's presence in the *Stromata* is a small but real acknowledgment of the universality of human spiritual capacity.

  

What it prevented: Clement did not have access to the Buddhist texts. He did not know the dharma in any philosophically meaningful sense. He knew the name and the reputation. He could not engage with the Buddhist account of suffering and its cessation, with the analysis of the self in the Abhidhamma, with the subtle philosophy of the Prajnaparamita tradition that was, in Clement's own lifetime, being developed in the Gandharan world whose products were traveling westward along the same routes that carried Roman glass to Begram. The Buddha in the *Stromata* is a name on a list, not a voice in a dialogue.

  

The dialogue would have to wait. But the name had arrived. The tradition had been acknowledged. A Christian theologian in Alexandria had written the word *Boutta* in a serious theological work and had found it intelligible — had fit it into a framework that could make sense of it, even if the sense it made was not entirely the sense the Buddhist tradition would have recognized in itself.

  

And that acknowledgment — however partial, however filtered through Clement's own theological categories — was the first footprint of what would eventually become one of the most extraordinary stories of religious transmission in the ancient world: the story of how the life of Siddhartha Gautama traveled along the routes of transmission, was translated and retranslated, was transformed by each cultural context through which it passed, and arrived eventually in the libraries of medieval Christian Europe as the life of a Christian saint named Josaphat.

  

The footprint is small. The journey it inaugurates is enormous.

  

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

**Clement of Alexandria,** ***Stromata*** (c. 200 CE). The first mention of the Buddha (Βούττα) by name in Christian literature. Book I, Chapter 15. The full passage distinguishes *Brahmins*, *Sarmanae* (śramaṇas), and the specific followers of *Boutta*.

  

**Porphyry,** ***On Abstinence from Animal Food*****, Book IV** (c. 3rd century CE). Extensive quotation from Bardesanes's account of Indian ascetic sects — the Brahmins and the Samanaeans — drawn from Bardesanes's direct meeting with an Indian delegation to Caesar.

  

**Bardesanes of Edessa** (154–222 CE). The Syriac scholar who met Indian philosophers sent to the Roman court and transmitted their account to the Syriac-speaking world; his material reached the Greek-reading world through Porphyry's quotation.

### Modern Scholarly Works

**McEvilley, Thomas.** ***The Shape of Ancient Thought*****.** Chapters 29–30: Clement of Alexandria and Indian philosophy; the Bardesanes transmission.

  

**Sedlar, Jean W.** ***India and the Greek World*****. Rowman & Littlefield, 1980.** For the broader context of Indian-Mediterranean philosophical exchange in the Hellenistic and early Roman periods.

  

**Drijvers, Han J.W.** ***Bardaisan of Edessa*****. Van Gorcum, 1966.** The standard scholarly treatment of Bardesanes and his significance as a transmitter of Indian knowledge.

### Threads Carried Forward

**→ Chapter 17** (Monasticism Without Borders) examines the structural parallels between Indian asceticism and early Christian monasticism — arguing that the Desert Fathers' practice was shaped, at however many removes, by the gymnosophist tradition and by the institutional model of communities like the Therapeutae. The gnosis that Clement's theological project was circling around, and the theosis that the Desert Fathers were pursuing, and the prajna that the Buddhist contemplative tradition had been developing for five centuries — these three terms are the chapter's argument.
