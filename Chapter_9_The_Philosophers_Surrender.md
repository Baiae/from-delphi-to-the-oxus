# Chapter 9 — The Philosopher-King's Surrender

  

*"King of the city of Euthymedia... learned, eloquent, wise, and able."* — *Milindapañha* (The Questions of King Milinda), introduction of Menander I

  

## I. The Coins Tell the Story First

Before we meet the man in dialogue, we meet him in metal.

  

The coins of Menander I are among the most eloquent objects to have survived from the ancient world. They were minted by the hundreds of thousands — in silver and in bronze, in the river towns and garrison cities of a kingdom that stretched from the Hindu Kush to the Ganges plain — and they bear, on their obverse face, the portrait of a king who looks directly out at the viewer with an expression that is difficult to categorize. Not the hieratic calm of a god-king. Not the martial ferocity of a conqueror. Something more interesting: intelligence. The face of a man who has been thinking, and who intends to continue.

  

On one side of the coin, in the Greek alphabet: ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΣ ΣΩΤΗΡΟΣ ΜΕΝΑΝΔΡΟΥ. King. Savior. Menander. On the other side, in Kharoshthi script: *Maharajasa Tratarasa Menadrasa*. The same words, the same man, in two languages and two alphabets that had never before shared a surface. And in the corner of the reverse — sometimes — an eight-spoked wheel. The Dharmachakra. The Wheel of the Law. On the same coin as Athena Alkidemos, Athena the Protector of the People, hurling her thunderbolt.

  

He was the most powerful of the Indo-Greek kings. His kingdom, built from the fragmented remnants of the Greco-Bactrian world, was by the mid-second century BCE one of the most formidable states in the subcontinent. His capital was at Sagala — modern Sialkot in the Punjab — and his reach was extraordinary: there is evidence of his military campaigns as far east as Pataliputra, the old Mauryan capital on the Ganges. He called himself *Soter* — Savior — an epithet with a specific history in the Greek tradition. Ptolemy I had used it. It carried the suggestion not merely of military deliverance but of something more: a king who did not merely conquer but preserved, who turned victory into something his subjects could live within.

  

His coins were found, centuries later, in places as distant as Britain — carried there by merchants or collectors who prized them for their artistry before anyone knew whose face they bore. They are beautiful objects. The man who minted them understood the power of beautiful objects to make arguments.

  

And he had been arguing for years. Not with enemies. He argued with monks.

  

## II. What Made Him Ready

The *Milindapañha* — "The Questions of King Milinda" — introduces Menander with a phrase that Buddhist hagiography reserves for people it genuinely means to praise: *learned, eloquent, wise, and able*. But the text does something more interesting than praise him. It specifies what he knew.

  

He was skilled, the *Milindapañha* tells us, in the Eighteen Sciences: not just the military and administrative arts expected of a king, but dialectics, mathematics, astronomy, the Vedas, the different systems of Indian philosophy. He was, by the standard of the ancient world, a man of comprehensive education — a man who had been trained to argue.

  

This was not coincidental to what happened when he met Nāgasena. It was the precondition for it.

  

The Greek philosophical tradition in which Menander had been formed — however he received it, whether through tutors or through the remarkable library culture of the Hellenistic cities, through the kind of education that a king in the Greco-Bactrian world would have considered proper for his station — had been building, for three centuries, toward something it could not quite reach. The Eleatics had argued that the self we encounter in ordinary experience is not what it appears to be. Heraclitus had argued that everything flows and nothing persists: you cannot step into the same river twice. The Skeptics, in the tradition initiated by Pyrrho of Elis — who had, famously, traveled with Alexander to India and spent time with Indian gymnosophists — had argued that definite assertions about the ultimate nature of things, including the ultimate nature of the self, cannot be justified. Pyrrho had come home from India with something. He had become, in the Greek tradition's memory, the philosopher who had achieved *ataraxia* — unperturbedness, an equanimity that the tradition associated with the highest form of philosophical attainment.

  

This was the intellectual inheritance Menander brought to his conversations with monks. He had not been reading it as preparation for meeting a Buddhist. But it had given him a mind supple enough to recognize, when the Buddhist arguments arrived, not their foreignness but their familiarity.

  

## III. The Teacher Behind the Teacher

There is a detail in the *Milindapañha* that the text mentions almost in passing, as background information, and that has not received anything like the attention it deserves.

  

Nāgasena — the monk who would answer Menander's questions, who would demonstrate the non-existence of the self with such devastating precision that Menander would acknowledge his intellectual surrender — was not educated by an Indian teacher. His first and most formative teacher in Buddhist philosophy was a monk named Dhammarakhita.

  

Dhammarakhita was Greek.

  

Let that settle for a moment. The teacher who formed Nāgasena — who gave him the philosophical tools and the argumentative rigor that would later astonish the most philosophically sophisticated king in the Hellenistic east — was himself a product of the same Greek tradition that had shaped Menander. We do not know his birth name. We do not know where exactly he was born, though his name and context suggest the Bactrian or Indo-Greek world. What we know is that he was a Greek Buddhist monk of sufficient learning and standing to train one of the tradition's most celebrated intellects — and that the training he gave Nāgasena bore the marks of its origin.

  

The chariot analogy that Nāgasena uses to demonstrate *anattā* — the non-self — is not just a convenient illustration. It is a specifically philosophical argument in the Greek analytical tradition: the technique of decomposing an apparent unity into its constituent parts and asking whether the name we give the whole refers to anything that is not simply the sum of the parts and their relations. Zeno had used variants of this technique. The Stoics would use it. It was, in Nāgasena's hands, Greek philosophical method applied to Buddhist content — which is to say: it was Dhammarakhita's gift to his student, passed forward into the dialogue that would change the most powerful king in the Indo-Greek world.

  

When Menander encountered Nāgasena, he was not encountering a foreign tradition. He was encountering his own tradition, transformed.

  

## IV. The Questions Begin

The *Milindapañha* is one of the strangest and most beautiful texts in the Buddhist canon. It is framed as a philosophical dialogue in the manner of Socratic exchange: the king poses questions, the monk responds, the king objects, the monk refines his answer. Back and forth, through dozens of questions over what the text implies were many sessions, perhaps many years.

  

The questions are Menander's real philosophical problems. He is not performing — the text has the quality of a genuine inquiry, a mind that cannot rest until it has found an answer that actually holds.

  

The first great question is disarmingly simple.

  

*Who is Nāgasena?*

  

It sounds trivial. It is not.

  

Menander asks the monk for his name. Nāgasena gives it. Menander then asks: what, exactly, is *Nāgasena*? Is Nāgasena his body? Nāgasena denies it. Is he his sensations? No. His perceptions? No. His mental formations? No. His consciousness? No. Is he the combination of all of these? No. Is he apart from all of these? No.

  

Menander's frustration is evident in the text. He presses: *then where is Nāgasena? Where is the person I am speaking to?*

  

Nāgasena answers with an analogy that has become one of the most famous in the history of philosophy.

  

## V. The Chariot and the Self

*Tell me, great king,* Nāgasena says. *You came here by chariot. Now — is the pole the chariot? Is the axle the chariot? Is the wheel the chariot? Is the frame the chariot? Is the yoke the chariot? Are all of these together the chariot? Or is the chariot something apart from all of these?*

  

Menander answers each question: no, no, no.

  

*Then is there a chariot at all?*

  

Menander sees it. *The chariot is a conventional designation. It is the name we give to the assemblage of parts and their functional relationship. It is not a thing-in-itself, apart from its parts. It is a concept, a useful label.*

  

*Exactly so,* says Nāgasena. *And thus is it with Nāgasena. By reason of these component parts — body, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness — I say "Nāgasena." But ultimately, upon close examination, there is no self to be found.*

  

This is the Buddhist doctrine of *anattā* — non-self. The denial that there is, anywhere in human experience, a fixed, permanent, unitary self that persists through time and underlies the flux of experience. What we call the self is a process, a convention, a useful fiction applied to a constantly changing stream of physical and mental events.

  

Menander, trained in the tradition that ran from Pythagoras through Socrates through Plato — a tradition in which the soul was the most real and most essential thing about a human being — understood immediately what he was being told. And he did not reject it.

  

But the chariot was only the first move. Menander was not satisfied. He pressed further.

  

If there is no self — no continuous entity that persists from one moment to the next — how does rebirth work? What is it that transmigrates? What is reborn, if not a self?

  

Nāgasena lit a lamp.

  

If I were to light a flame from this flame, he asked, is the second flame the same as the first, or different?

  

Neither the same nor different, said Menander, already following the logic.

  

Just so with rebirth, said Nāgasena. There is continuity without identity. The flame that burns in my lamp now is not the flame that was burning an hour ago — not in any philosophically strict sense — and yet there is an unbroken causal chain from that earlier flame to this one. The process continues. The entity does not. What is reborn is not a self carried intact from one life to the next. It is a process — a stream of conditioned arising, of cause producing effect, of karma generating consequence — that continues without requiring a fixed entity to carry it.

  

Menander had read enough philosophy to follow this. The Stoics had argued for a universe of immanent causation — a cosmos held together not by the will of personal gods but by the rational principle of *logos*, the impersonal ground of all causal order. The atomists had argued that what we call individual things are temporary configurations of particles, that dissolution and reconfiguration are the fundamental processes of reality. The soul, in these traditions, was not obviously the permanent, separable entity that the Platonic tradition required.

  

Nāgasena was not contradicting everything Menander had been taught. He was completing it.

  

## VI. Two Traditions at the Same Table

What makes the *Milindapañha* historically extraordinary is not that a Greek king is talking to a Buddhist monk. By the mid-second century BCE, this was not particularly unusual. The extraordinary thing is the *quality* of the conversation — the fact that Menander actually understands what Nāgasena is saying, actually follows the argument, and is actually persuaded by it.

  

This did not happen by accident. It happened because the Greek philosophical tradition had been preparing Menander, for his entire life, for exactly this conversation.

  

When Nāgasena demonstrated the emptiness of the self through the chariot analogy, he was using the same method that Socrates used in the *Phaedo* — taking apart an apparent unity and showing that the unity was conceptual, conventional, not metaphysically real. When he refused to assert either that the self exists or that it does not exist after death — the classic Buddhist refusal to answer the "undetermined questions" — he was doing something that Pyrrho of Elis, trained among Indian gymnosophists, would have recognized immediately.

  

The two traditions were not strangers at this table. They had been moving toward each other for centuries. And at this table, in this dialogue, they met in a way that neither tradition had quite managed on its own: Nāgasena could answer questions that Pyrrho's tradition had only formulated, could supply a path where the Greek philosophical tradition had only supplied an insight. The Buddhist tradition had, centuries of meditative practice had, developed techniques for working *with* the reality of non-self — for inhabiting it, not merely assenting to it. What Menander was being offered was not a better argument but a different kind of thing altogether: a practice. A way through.

  

## VII. The Surrender

The *Milindapañha* builds toward a moment that the text stages with deliberate care.

  

After the questions — the chariot, the nature of the self, the mechanism of rebirth, the meaning of *nirvāna*, the relationship between wisdom and virtue — Menander acknowledges what has happened. He has not been defeated in argument in any ordinary sense. He has been *educated*. His questions have been answered. His objections have been met. The philosophical tradition he brought to the conversation has been shown — not contradicted, but shown — to be incomplete: capable of approaching the truth but not quite reaching it, needing something that only the Buddhist tradition, in Nāgasena's presentation of it, was supplying.

  

The text records his declaration: *"May the venerable Nāgasena accept me as a supporter of the faith, from this day forth while my life shall last."*

  

Whether the historical Menander actually converted to Buddhism — whether he became a Buddhist in the full, formal, institutional sense — remains a matter of scholarly debate. His coins continue to show Greek deities alongside the Dharmachakra. He does not appear to have renounced his throne or become a monk. But Buddhist tradition remembers him as one of the great benefactors of the faith, ranking him alongside Ashoka and Kanishka in the list of kings who made Buddhism flourish. And some accounts do suggest that in the end, in the final years of his reign, he withdrew from the world entirely — that the philosopher-king found his way, however gradually, to the philosopher's surrender.

  

There is one more detail that the Greek tradition preserves, and it is worth pausing over. Plutarch, writing in the first century CE from his study in Chaeronea, records a curious tradition about Menander's death. When Menander died — in his military camp, according to most accounts, on campaign — the towns of his kingdom, says Plutarch, disputed possession of his remains. They competed for his ashes. And the settlement, Plutarch tells us, was the same settlement that Buddhist tradition records was made for the Buddha's own remains after his *parinirvāna*: the ashes were divided, and each town received a portion to enshrine.

  

Plutarch was writing about a Greek king. The Buddhists were writing about the Buddha. The ritual was the same.

  

Whether this is coincidence or imitation — whether the Buddhists modeled the Buddha's funeral on the tradition of the heroes, or whether Menander's followers modeled his funeral on the Buddhist tradition of his adopted faith, or whether both traditions were drawing independently on a shared Indo-Iranian funerary custom — is a question that cannot now be answered with certainty. But the parallel, preserved in a Greek text about a Greek king, suggests something: that by the time Menander died, the distinction between what was Greek about him and what was Buddhist about him had become, for his people, difficult to locate.

  

## VIII. Transformation, Not Persuasion

There is something more precise to be said about what Menander's recognition consisted of — something that the word *persuasion* does not quite capture.

  

Menander was not persuaded by Nāgasena in the way that a juror is persuaded by an advocate, following an argument from premise to conclusion and finding the logic sound. Something different happened. He was *transformed* by the encounter. The chariot argument did not convince him; it broke something open in him. He had arrived at the dialogue with a philosophical tradition and a trained intelligence and a genuine willingness to engage. But he could not infer his way to the place Nāgasena was pointing. No sequence of valid arguments, however carefully constructed, could deposit him there.

  

Understanding the emptiness of the self is not something you arrive at by following a chain of reasoning to its end. It is something you undergo. The Greek philosophical tradition had known this, in its best moments: Plato had written dialogues rather than treatises because dialogue — the living, responsive exchange between minds — could do what systematic exposition could not. The *Phaedo*, the *Republic*, the *Symposium* were not arguments to be concluded; they were experiences to be had. The form was as important as the content.

  

The *Milindapañha* belongs to this tradition. It was written as a dialogue because Nāgasena's wisdom could not be extracted from the exchange and summarized in propositions without being lost. The king who asked the questions had to ask them himself. The monk who answered them had to answer this particular king. The conversation was the thing.

  

The Greeks had a word for the commitment that Menander's declaration represents. It is not the word for wisdom acquired, or knowledge gained, or argument concluded. It is the word for the beginning of a path whose end one cannot yet see — the *loving* of wisdom, *philosophia*, that commits to a transformation one cannot fully anticipate. The *Milindapañha* records that commitment. Whatever Menander became after it — and the historical record is, as always in this territory, incomplete — he had done something that the Greek philosophical tradition had long honored in theory and rarely managed in practice: he had recognized, in an encounter with a form of wisdom different from his own, the thing his own wisdom had been reaching toward.

  

He had turned toward it.

  

That is what the *Milindapañha* means when it says that the king became a *supporter of the faith*. Not a label adopted. Not a political calculation. The word it uses — the concept it reaches for — is the word for someone who has committed to *becoming*. Menander had met his teacher's teacher's tradition in its fullness, embodied in a monk who had himself been shaped by a Greek, and he had recognized it.

  

## IX. What the Text Is

One more thing must be said about the *Milindapañha* itself, because the text is as remarkable as its subject.

  

It is not a simple record of a conversation. It is a literary achievement of the highest order — a philosophical dialogue composed with the awareness of what philosophical dialogue, at its best, can do. The questions it poses are not rhetorical. The answers it provides are not merely reassuring. The monk does not always immediately answer the king's questions — sometimes he redirects, sometimes he uses a counter-question, sometimes he reaches for an analogy from ordinary life that reframes the whole problem. The king does not merely accept every answer — sometimes he pushes back, sometimes he finds the analogy insufficient, sometimes he proposes alternative framings that force Nāgasena to refine his position.

  

The form is Socratic. The content is Buddhist. The interlocutors are, in terms of their respective cultural formations, the most rigorously prepared minds that each tradition could have produced. And what they made together — this text, these questions, this record of a mind being changed — is one of the most important documents in the history of human thought.

  

Not because it changed the course of an empire. Not because it settled a philosophical question that had been disputed for centuries. But because it demonstrated, concretely, that the dialogue between these two traditions was *real*. That the synthesis was not merely aesthetic — not just Greek faces on Buddhist statues — but philosophical, reaching into the deepest questions of what exists, what the self is, and how suffering might be brought to an end.

  

This was never separate.

  

## Sources & Bibliographical Notes

### Primary Ancient Sources

***Milindapañha*** (The Questions of King Milinda) (c. 1st century BCE–1st century CE; Pali canon). The primary text of this chapter. The standard English translation is T.W. Rhys Davids, *The Questions of King Milinda* (Sacred Books of the East, vols. 35–36, 1890–1894), still readable and reliable.

  

**Greek sources on Menander**: Strabo, *Geography*, XI.11.1; Plutarch, *Moralia* ("On the Fortune of Alexander"), 5. Both confirm information about the Indo-Greek Buddhist king was circulating throughout the Hellenistic world. Plutarch's account of the division of Menander's ashes among the cities of his kingdom is the crucial detail, preserved at *Moralia* 821D.

### Modern Scholarly Works

**Rhys Davids, T.W., trans.** ***The Questions of King Milinda*****. Clarendon Press, 1890–94 (2 vols.).** The foundational English scholarly translation. Still the standard reference.

  

**Narain, A.K.** ***The Indo-Greeks*****. Oxford University Press, 1957.** Standard modern historical account of the Indo-Greek kingdoms; essential for Menander's biography and coinage.

  

**McEvilley, Thomas.** ***The Shape of Ancient Thought*****. Allworth Press, 2002.** Chapter 19: the most detailed comparative philosophical analysis of the *Milindapañha*, including the parallels with Pyrrhonism and the Eleatics.

  

**Stoneman, Richard.** ***The Greek Experience of India*****. Princeton University Press, 2019.** Chapter 10: situates the Menander-Nāgasena dialogue within the broader history of Greek-Indian intellectual exchange.

  

**Boardman, John.** ***The Greeks in Asia*****. Thames & Hudson, 2015.** Numismatic context for Menander's coinage and the Athena Alkidemos/Dharmachakra synthesis.
